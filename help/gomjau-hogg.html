GomJau-Hogg Notation

A GomJau-Hogg code is one short line of text that describes a tiling of the plane by regular polygons, like 6-4-3/m30/r(c2) for the tiling of hexagons, squares and triangles. The sims with a Lattice menu (Site Percolation and Bond Percolation) take any code in the box under the menu, so they can run on any tiling you can write down. The notation is by Gómez-Jauregui, Hogg, Manchado and Otero [1]; the code that reads it here is this site's own, written from their paper.

How a code reads

A code is pieces separated by slashes. The first piece places a few polygons; each piece after it is a move that copies everything so far somewhere else. Then the moves are done again and again, forever, and the polygons fill the plane. Every polygon has sides of length 1, and it can have 3, 4, 6, 8 or 12 sides, the only regular polygons that appear in tilings like these [2, Section 2.1].

Take 6-4-3/m30/r(c2). Step through it in the box above with the arrows.

  1. 6-4-3: a hexagon in the middle, a square on one of its sides, and a triangle on one of the square's sides.
  2. m30: mirror all of that across lines through the middle of the plane, 30 degrees apart. Now there is a hexagon ringed by squares and triangles.
  3. r(c2): a half turn around point c2, the middle of a polygon. This puts a copy of the ring next to the first one.

Repeating the two moves forever fills the plane.

1. The polygons

  • The first number is the polygon in the middle. It sits with two of its sides upright, its middle at the middle of the plane. (A triangle instead has its left side upright, going up from the middle of the plane, and points right.)
  • Each dash starts a new phase (the polygon in the middle is phase 1). Its polygons go on the free sides of the polygons placed in the phase before (sides no other polygon has yet), one polygon per side, taking the sides in order going clockwise from straight up. Polygons in one phase are separated by commas: 4-3,3 is a square with triangles on its first two free sides.
  • A 0 skips a free side: 4-0,3 puts the triangle on the square's second free side.

2. The moves

MoveWhat it does
r60Turn everything around the middle of the plane by 60 degrees, then 120, then 240 (doubling while under 360), keeping every copy. The angle can be 30, 45, 60, 90, 120 or 180; plain r is r180.
m30The same with mirrors: mirror across the line through the middle 30 degrees clockwise from straight up, then 60, then 120, then 240.
r(h2)A half turn around a point: here h2, the 2nd middle of a side.
m(h2)A mirror through a point. Through the middle of a side, the mirror runs along the side. Through a corner or the middle of a polygon, it runs at right angles to the line from the middle of the plane.

The points are of three kinds: c for the middle of a polygon (its centroid), v for a corner (a vertex), and h for the middle of a side (a half-way point). Each kind is numbered from 1, going clockwise from straight up, and at the same angle the nearer point comes first; the middle of the plane itself doesn't count. The numbers are worked out again before each move, from everything placed so far. That is what the "Step by step" picture shows: the points numbered there are the ones the next move can use.

3. Forever

After the last move, all the moves are done again, in order, and again, forever. The tiling is everything the moves carry the first polygons to. If the copies land on top of each other, or leave gaps that no copy fills, the code isn't a tiling, and the line under the code box says what went wrong.

Tips

  • Build a code a piece at a time: type the polygons, look at the numbered points, add a move, and so on. The examples menu has a code for each tiling in the sims' Lattice menu.
  • A tiling has many codes. Turns and mirrors around the middle (like m30) usually come first, then moves around points (like r(h2)) that carry copies away from the middle.
  • If the line under the box says the code "doesn't repeat", the moves only spin things around a point and never move them away: add a move around a point further out.

Where this comes from

The paper [1] lists codes for 90 tilings in its Table 1. This site's reader follows the paper's rules, and where the paper doesn't say exactly what to do (which of two sides at the same angle comes first, say), it does what the authors' own program, ANTWERP, does. For two of the tilings in the Lattice menu the reader builds a different tiling from the code in the paper's table, so the menu uses other codes for those. The Percolation check page builds every code in the menu and in the paper's table and checks the kinds of corner each one has.
