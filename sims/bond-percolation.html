About the model

Every pair of neighboring cells is joined by an edge, and every edge flips its own coin: it is open with probability p and closed otherwise, all independently. Cells joined by open edges form clusters, each drawn in its own color (open edges are lines, cells are dots; zoomed far out, each cell becomes a square in its cluster's color).

For small p the clusters are tiny. As p grows they join up, and on a big grid there is a sharp change at the critical point pc: below it every cluster is small, above it one giant cluster spreads through the whole domain. On the square grid with 4 neighbors, pc is exactly 1/2, one of the famous theorems of probability.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. Percolation also runs on a ball of a hyperbolic tiling or of a tree: every cell within R steps of a start cell (the one with the ring). It is drawn in the Poincaré disk or the half-plane (drag to move around the plane, or press Slide to slide the whole picture), or, for a tree, spread out in rings, and all three zoom. It is drawn as the graph itself: each open edge in its cluster's color, and the closed ones in thin gray. Statistics then say whether the start's cluster reaches the edge of the ball. On a tree where every cell has d neighbors, pc = 1/(d − 1). The hyperbolic plane has two critical points: between them there are infinitely many infinite clusters at once, and above the second exactly one.

Things to try: drag the p slider, or press Play to raise p slowly and watch clusters swallow each other (a cluster keeps its color when it swallows a smaller one). At p = 1/2, try a box one cell wider than tall: a cluster crosses from left to right exactly half the time over many seeds. Click a cluster to follow it.

The model. The domain is a graph G = (V, E): the cells, joined to their 4 or 8 neighbors. A configuration is \omega \in \{0,1\}^E, with \omega(e) = 1 meaning edge e is open. Under \mathbb{P}_p the \omega(e) are independent Bernoulli(p). A cluster is a connected component of the graph (V, \{e : \omega(e) = 1\}), so a cell with no open edge is a cluster of one cell [1, Chapter 1].

The coupling. Each edge gets one uniform number U_e \in [0,1) from the seed, and

\omega_p(e) = \mathbf{1}\{U_e < p\}.

For each p this has law \mathbb{P}_p, and \omega_p \le \omega_{p'} for p \le p', so clusters only grow and merge as p rises [1, Chapter 1]. This is why the slider morphs the picture.

Every p at once. Opening the edges one at a time in order of U_e and merging clusters with union-find gives the exact largest cluster and number of clusters of this sample for every p in one pass (the Newman–Ziff algorithm [2]); those are the two curves in Statistics. Union-find with path compression and union by size takes almost constant time per step [3].

The phase transition. On the infinite square grid let \theta(p) be the probability that the origin's cluster is infinite, and p_c = \sup\{p : \theta(p) = 0\}. Below p_c cluster sizes have an exponential tail [1, Chapters 5 and 6], so the largest cluster in a domain of n cells has only about \log n cells; above it there is exactly one infinite cluster [1, Chapter 8], and the largest cluster fills about a fraction \theta(p) of the domain.

Exactly 1/2. With 4 neighbors, \theta(1/2) = 0 (Harris [4]) and p_c = 1/2 (Kesten [5]; see [1, Chapter 11]). The reason is duality: put a dual cell in every square of the grid, and let a dual edge be closed exactly when the edge it crosses is closed. On the box with n + 1 columns and n rows of cells, exactly one of these happens: an open crossing from left to right, or a closed dual crossing from top to bottom. The dual box is the same shape turned on its side, and at p = 1/2 open and closed look alike, so each happens with probability exactly 1/2 [1, Chapter 11; 6, Chapter 3]. The check page tests both facts.

Clusters per cell. The number of clusters per cell tends to a smooth function \kappa(p) as the domain grows [1, Chapter 4]. At the critical point of the 4-neighbor grid it is known exactly:

\kappa(1/2) = \frac{3\sqrt{3} - 5}{2} \approx 0.0981

[7, 8]. With 8 neighbors there is no exact value of p_c, so no dashed line is drawn.

Crossing and wrapping. On a box, a cluster crosses when it touches both the left and the right column. On a torus, the sim says whether a cluster wraps all the way around, found as in [2]: each cell remembers where it sits in the unwrapped plane, and a cluster wraps when two of its cells meet across an edge in different copies.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. Here G is the ball B_R = \{ v : d(o, v) \le R \} of a regular tiling \{p, q\} of the hyperbolic plane (here the p of \{p, q\} is the number of sides, not the probability; 1/p + 1/q < 1/2, and neighbors share a side) or of the regular tree of degree d, with d(o, v) the number of steps from the start cell o, and only the edges inside the ball. The sim says whether o's cluster reaches the edge \partial B_R = \{ v : d(o, v) = R \}.

On the tree, the start's cluster is a branching process: every cell in it other than o has d - 1 children, each joined to it by an open edge with probability p, independently. So \theta(p) > 0 exactly when (d - 1) p > 1, and p_c = 1/(d - 1) [9, Chapter 5]. The same reasoning gives the chance of reaching the edge exactly: with the probability that a cell is joined to the cells k levels below it,

f_0 = 1, \qquad f_k = 1 - (1 - p f_{k-1})^{d-1}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow \partial B_R) = 1 - (1 - p f_{R-1})^{d},

and the check page compares the sim with it.

On a hyperbolic tiling there are two critical points, 0 < p_c < p_u < 1: for p \le p_c there is no infinite cluster, for p_c < p < p_u there are infinitely many, and for p \ge p_u exactly one (Benjamini and Schramm [10]). No exact values are known, so no dashed line is drawn there.
