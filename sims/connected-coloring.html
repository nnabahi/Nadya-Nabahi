About the model

The domain is split into N colors, and each color is one connected piece: you can walk between any two of its cells without leaving the color.

Each move merges two neighboring colors into one region and splits it again at random: a random tree is drawn through the region, visiting every cell, and one of its links is cut. The tree falls into two connected pieces, and those are the two new colors.

Some moves are refused, with just the right probability that, in the long run, every such coloring is equally likely. The other tab explains why, with references.

Things to try: press Play (or Step, for one move at a time); change N; switch to the torus (drag it and it wraps around; zoom with the mouse wheel, a pinch, or the + and − buttons); or pick Custom to draw your own region and even your own starting colors.

A connected N-coloring of the domain gives every cell one of N colors so that each color is used and forms one connected piece. This sim moves randomly between them in such a way that, in the long run, every connected N-coloring is equally likely.

One move (ReCom, short for recombination [1]), from the current coloring X:

  1. Pick two colors i, j that touch, uniformly among the A(X) pairs that touch.
  2. Merge them: M = C_i \cup C_j.
  3. Draw a uniformly random spanning tree of M with Wilson's algorithm [2].
  4. Cut a uniformly random edge of the tree. It falls into two connected pieces, P and Q.
  5. A fair coin decides which piece gets color i; the other gets j. Call this Y.
  6. Move to Y with probability
    \alpha = \min\left(1,\ \frac{A(X)}{A(Y)} \cdot \frac{\tau(C_i)\,\tau(C_j)}{\tau(P)\,\tau(Q)} \cdot \frac{k(C_i, C_j)}{k(P, Q)}\right),
    otherwise stay at X. Here \tau(S) is the number of spanning trees of S, and k(S, R) the number of edges joining S to R.

Why every coloring is equally likely. A spanning tree of M with an edge that splits it into \{P, Q\} is the same thing as a spanning tree of P, one of Q, and an edge joining them. So steps 1–5 propose Y with probability

q(X \to Y) = \frac{1}{A(X)} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\tau(P)\,\tau(Q)\,k(P, Q)}{\tau(M)\,(|M| - 1)},

and the way back merges the same M. Accepting with \min(1, q(Y \to X) / q(X \to Y)), which is \alpha above, is the Metropolis–Hastings rule for the uniform distribution [3, 4]: the chain is reversible with respect to it, so the uniform distribution is stationary. (The long-run claim also needs every connected coloring to be reachable from every other; that is not proved here for every domain.) The coin matters: without it, which piece gets color i depends on where the tree was rooted, and q would not be the formula above.

Counting spanning trees. By Kirchhoff's Matrix-Tree Theorem [5, 6], \tau(S) is the determinant of the Laplacian matrix of S with one row and the matching column removed. It is computed (as a logarithm, since it is huge) with a banded Cholesky factorization [7], numbering the cells in breadth-first order so the matrix is banded. The count for each color is remembered, so a move needs only two new determinants.

Domains and starts. A box or torus of cells, with 4 or 8 neighbors, or your own region drawn in the graph tool. The torus can be moved by dragging it and zoomed with the mouse wheel, a pinch or the buttons in its corner; zoomed out, it shows several times side by side. The automatic start cuts the domain into a grid of N rectangles; a drawn region that can't be cut that way with each color in one piece starts from N compact blocks around spread-out cells instead. Compact pieces matter: from thin stripes, almost every move would be refused at first. With a custom domain you can also paint your own start. The seed fixes the random numbers, so a run can be repeated exactly.
