About the model

Blocks are dropped one at a time and pile up into a mountain. The base is the set of sites that already have a block. A block may land on any site of the base, or on a site the tile reaches from the base. It picks one of these available sites completely at random, all equally likely.

With the tile {−1, +1} on a line, a block lands on the base or one site past either end, so the base only grows when a block lands just past an end. Most blocks land on the base, and the mountain gets taller much faster than it gets wider.

Things to try: the one-sided tile {+1}; a tile with a gap, like {−2, +1}; 2D with 4 or 8 neighbors; a tile of your own, clicked in the grid; a thin ring, which spreads more than it stacks; or a small domain, where the base soon fills it and the mountain only gets taller. On a 2D torus, drag the picture to slide it around, and zoom with the mouse wheel, a pinch, or the + and − buttons; zoomed out, the torus shows several times side by side.

The rule. The tile T is a finite set of offsets in \mathbb{Z}^d (d = 1 or 2). The base and the available sites start as

D_0 = \{0\}, \qquad S_0 = \{0\} \cup T,

with one block on 0: H(0, 0) = 1. At each step n = 1, 2, \dots:

X_n \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(S_{n-1}), \qquad H(X_n, n) = H(X_n, n-1) + 1,
D_n = D_{n-1} \cup \{X_n\}, \qquad S_n = S_{n-1} \cup (X_n + T).

So S_n = \{0\} \cup (D_n + T), and after n steps there are n + 1 blocks. The uniform choice of X_n is the only randomness. In a domain \Omega, sites outside \Omega are never available; on a torus or a cycle the offsets wrap around.

nadya's models are presets: T = \{-1, +1\} is the two-sided mountain (randmountain.py, RandBlockStack.py), where X_n is uniform on the base interval or one site past either end; T = \{+1\} is the one-sided mountain (randonesidedmountain.py); and the 4 neighbors in 2D is the 2D mountain. For the two-sided mountain the base is an interval of L sites, and it grows at the next step with probability 2/(L+2).

The base is an Eden model. Given that the base grows at a step, the new site is uniform on S_{n-1} \setminus D_{n-1} = (D_{n-1} + T) \setminus D_{n-1}, the outer boundary of the base. So the base, watched only at the steps where it grows, is Eden's growth model [1] with the neighbors given by T; the mountain adds the heights on top.

Random numbers. Step n uses a uniform number u_n \in [0, 1) and picks site number \lfloor u_n |S_{n-1}| \rfloor, as in randmountain.py. In 1D the sites are counted from left to right, as in the Python, so the same u's give exactly the same mountain; in 2D they are counted in the order they became available. The u_n come from the seeded generator seedrandom [2], and the same seed gives the same u_n whatever the tile and domain.

References
