About the model

N random walkers wander around the domain, each with its own color. Every cell keeps the color of the first walker to land on it, and keeps it for good. The run ends when every cell is colored.

Each step, a walker moves to one of its neighboring cells, chosen at random. In discrete time all walkers step together, and if several land on a new cell at once, one of them is picked at random to color it. In continuous time each walker has its own random clock, and only one walker moves at a time.

The interface counts the places where two colors touch. A region is a connected piece of one color: a color can be cut into many regions, because another walker may get to some cells first.

Things to try: drag the walkers to new starting places, then press Play; switch between the two models; change N; or pick Custom to draw your own region. On the torus, drag the empty space to slide the picture around, and zoom with the mouse wheel, a pinch, or the + and − buttons; zoomed out, the torus shows several times side by side.

The domain is a graph G = (V, E): the cells, joined to their 4 or 8 neighbors (on a torus, the edges wrap around). Walker i, for i = 1, \dots, N, is at X^i_t and carries color i. Each step goes to a uniformly chosen neighbor, so each walker is a simple random walk on G. The color of cell v is C(v), with C(v) = 0 while no walker has been there.

Start. Every occupied cell takes the color of a uniformly chosen walker on it:

C(v) \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{\, i : X^i_0 = v \,\}.

Discrete time. At each step n = 1, 2, \dots all walkers move at once, independently. Then every uncolored occupied cell takes the color of a uniformly chosen walker on it:

C(v) \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{\, i : X^i_n = v \,\} \quad \text{if } C(v) = 0.

The uniform choice is made by putting the walkers in a uniformly random order (the Fisher–Yates shuffle [1]) and letting the first one on a cell take it. With 4 neighbors on a box, or on a torus with even sides, the graph is a checkerboard (bipartite) and every step changes a walker's square color; so two walkers can only ever meet on a cell if they started on the same color of the checkerboard.

Continuous time. Each walker steps when its own rate-1 exponential clock rings. The first of N independent \mathrm{Exp}(1) clocks rings after an \mathrm{Exp}(N) time, is equally likely to be any of them, and which one it is does not depend on when [2, Theorem 2.3.3]; by the memoryless property [2, Theorem 2.3.1] the clocks then start afresh. So the sim waits an \mathrm{Exp}(N) time, moves a uniformly chosen walker one step, and repeats. Two clocks never ring at once, so no ties need breaking after the start. Time t counts in the same units as the steps n: by time t each walker has made t steps on average.

Coupling. Each walker has its own stream of random numbers, used only for its own steps. With the same seed, the walkers follow exactly the same paths in both models; only the order in which the steps happen differs, and with it the coloring.

The statistics. The interface is the number of edges whose two cells have different colors (uncolored cells don't count), as in [3]. A region is a connected piece of one color, using the domain's own neighbors. Its area is its number of cells, and its perimeter the number of edges from it to a different color, so the perimeters add up to twice the interface. The cover time is when the last cell gets colored. One walker on an L \times L torus needs about \tfrac{4}{\pi} L^2 (\log L)^2 steps to visit every cell [4]; several walkers are faster [5].

Background. The one-dimensional version, two walkers competing on a cycle, is studied in [3]; see also [6].
