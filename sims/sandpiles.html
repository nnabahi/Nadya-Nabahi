About the model

Every cell holds some grains of sand. A cell with 4 or more grains (8 with 8 neighbors) is too tall: it topples, giving one grain to each of its neighbors. That can make the neighbors topple too, in an avalanche. Grains that fall off the edge of the table are lost. When no cell can topple, the pile is stable.

The next cell to topple is picked at random, but the final pile is always the same, whatever the order. That is why "Jump to the end" can skip straight to it.

On a torus there is no edge. With one sink cell, that cell eats every grain it gets. With no sink, grains can never leave: with too many grains the pile topples forever, and the sim says so.

The identity is a special stable pile: adding it to any pile that a long storm can make, and toppling, gives that pile back. Every table with a sink has one, often a beautiful picture. "Show the identity" shows it, and the start "Identity (watch it form)" lets you watch it topple into place.

Things to try: slide n for the center pile with "Jump to the end whenever a setting changes" ticked; start a storm and watch the avalanches; draw your own table and show its identity. On the torus, drag the picture to slide it around, and zoom with the mouse wheel, a pinch, or the + and − buttons.

The rule. The table is a graph: the cells V, joined to their 4 or 8 neighbors, plus a sink s. Each lattice neighbor of v that is not on the table (or is the sink cell) is an edge from v to s, so every cell has degree d(v) = 4 (or 8). A pile is \eta : V \to \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}. If \eta(v) \ge d(v), cell v may topple:

\eta(v) \mapsto \eta(v) - d(v), \qquad \eta(w) \mapsto \eta(w) + 1 \ \text{for each neighbor } w \ne s.

This is the abelian sandpile of Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld [1], in the form of Dhar [2]. In the sim, the next cell to topple is uniform among the unstable cells, as in nadya's own sandpile code; "All unstable cells at once" topples them together, as in [1].

The abelian property [2]. If every cell can reach the sink, toppling always stops, and the stable pile \eta^\circ and the number of topples at each cell do not depend on the order. "Jump to the end" uses this: a cell with \eta(v) grains topples \lfloor \eta(v)/d(v) \rfloor times at once.

No sink. On the torus without a sink, grains are conserved. Björner, Lovász and Shor [3] showed that if every cell topples at least once the toppling never ends, so the sim stops there and says so. With N grains, it always ends if N < |E| and never ends if N > 2|E| - |V|, where |E| counts the pairs of neighbors [3, Theorem 1.1].

Recurrent piles and the identity. Drop grains on uniformly random cells forever (the storm, waiting until stable): the piles that keep coming back are the recurrent ones. They form a group under "add, then topple", \eta \oplus \xi = (\eta + \xi)^\circ, the sandpile group, and the storm visits each of them equally often in the long run [2]. Their number is the determinant of the reduced Laplacian, which by Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem [4] is the number of spanning trees [2]. The sim checks recurrence with Dhar's burning test [2]: add one grain to each cell for each of its edges to the sink and topple; the pile is recurrent exactly when every cell topples once. The identity of the group is

e = \bigl(2M - (2M)^\circ\bigr)^\circ, \qquad M(v) = d(v) - 1,

on any table [5, 6]: 2M - (2M)^\circ differs from the empty pile only by topples and is at least M everywhere, so its stable version is recurrent and equivalent to the empty pile, and that is the identity. "Identity (watch it form)" starts from 2M - (2M)^\circ. The identity pictures on squares were made famous by Creutz [7].

Statistics. An avalanche is the number of topples caused by one grain of the storm. They follow a rough power law, the "self-organized criticality" of [1]. For recurrent piles on large squares, the fraction of cells with no grains tends to \tfrac{2}{\pi^2} - \tfrac{4}{\pi^3} \approx 0.074 [8]. The center pile of n grains, scaled down by \sqrt{n}, converges to a limit shape [9].
