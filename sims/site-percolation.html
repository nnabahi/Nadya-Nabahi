About the model

Every cell flips its own coin: it is open with probability p and closed (white) otherwise, all independently. Open cells that are neighbors stick together into clusters, each drawn in its own color.

For small p the clusters are tiny. As p grows they join up, and on a big grid there is a sharp change at the critical point pc: just below it every cluster is small, and just above it one giant cluster spreads through the whole domain. On the square grid with 4 neighbors, pc is about 0.5927, and nobody knows an exact formula for it.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. Percolation also runs on a ball of a hyperbolic tiling or of a tree: every cell within R steps of a start cell (the one with the ring). It is drawn in the Poincaré disk or the half-plane (drag to move around the plane), or, for a tree, spread out in rings, and all three zoom. Statistics then say whether the start's cluster reaches the edge of the ball. On a tree where every cell has d neighbors, pc = 1/(d − 1). The hyperbolic plane has two critical points: between them there are infinitely many infinite clusters at once, and above the second exactly one.

Things to try: drag the p slider, or press Play to raise p slowly and watch clusters swallow each other (a cluster keeps its color when it swallows a smaller one). Press p = pc and zoom in: the clusters look alike at every scale. Click a cluster to follow it. Switch to 8 neighbors and see pc move.

The model. The domain is a graph G = (V, E): the cells, joined to their 4 or 8 neighbors. A configuration is \omega \in \{0,1\}^V, with \omega(v) = 1 meaning cell v is open. Under \mathbb{P}_p the \omega(v) are independent Bernoulli(p). A cluster is a connected component of the open cells in G [1, Chapter 1].

The coupling. Each cell gets one uniform number U_v \in [0,1) from the seed, and

\omega_p(v) = \mathbf{1}\{U_v < p\}.

For each p this has law \mathbb{P}_p, and \omega_p \le \omega_{p'} for p \le p', so clusters only grow and merge as p rises [1, Chapter 1]. This is why the slider morphs the picture.

Every p at once. Opening the cells one at a time in order of U_v and merging clusters with union-find gives the exact largest cluster and number of clusters of this sample for every p in one pass (the Newman–Ziff algorithm [2]); those are the two curves in Statistics. Union-find with path compression and union by size takes almost constant time per step [3]. The number of clusters per cell tends to a smooth function \kappa(p) as the domain grows [1, Chapter 4].

The phase transition. On the infinite square grid let \theta(p) be the probability that the origin's cluster is infinite, and p_c = \sup\{p : \theta(p) = 0\}. Then 0 < p_c < 1 [1, Chapter 1]. Below p_c the size of a cluster has an exponential tail [1, Chapters 5 and 6], so in a domain of n cells the largest cluster has only about \log n cells; above it, there is exactly one infinite cluster [1, Chapter 8], and the largest cluster in the domain fills about a fraction \theta(p) of it.

The critical point. For 4 neighbors no exact value of p_c is known; the best estimate is p_c = 0.592746050792\ldots [4] (the dashed line uses 0.592746 [2]). The grid with 8 neighbors is the “matching lattice” of the grid with 4: a closed path with 8 neighbors blocks every open path with 4. So p_c^{(8)} = 1 - p_c^{(4)} \approx 0.407254 [5; 6, Chapter 3].

Crossing. On a box, a cluster crosses when it touches both the left and the right column. Exactly one of these happens: an open 4-neighbor crossing from left to right, or a closed 8-neighbor crossing from top to bottom (the same fact as “Hex never ends in a draw” [7]; see [6, Chapter 2]). The check page tests this on thousands of samples. On a torus, the sim instead says whether a cluster wraps all the way around, found as in [2]: each cell remembers where it sits in the unwrapped plane, and a cluster wraps when two of its cells meet across an edge in different copies.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. Here G is the ball B_R = \{ v : d(o, v) \le R \} of a regular tiling \{p, q\} of the hyperbolic plane (here the p of \{p, q\} is the number of sides, not the probability; 1/p + 1/q < 1/2, and neighbors share a side) or of the regular tree of degree d, with d(o, v) the number of steps from the start cell o, and only the edges inside the ball. The sim says whether o's cluster reaches the edge \partial B_R = \{ v : d(o, v) = R \}.

On the tree, the start's cluster is a branching process: every cell in it other than o has d - 1 children, each open with probability p, independently. So \theta(p) > 0 exactly when (d - 1) p > 1, and p_c = 1/(d - 1) [9, Chapter 5]. The same reasoning gives the chance of reaching the edge exactly: with the probability that a cell is open and joined to the cells k levels below it,

g_0 = p, \qquad g_k = p\,\big(1 - (1 - g_{k-1})^{d-1}\big), \qquad \mathbb{P}(o \leftrightarrow \partial B_R) = p\,\big(1 - (1 - g_{R-1})^{d}\big),

and the check page compares the sim with it.

On a hyperbolic tiling there are two critical points, 0 < p_c < p_u < 1: for p \le p_c there is no infinite cluster, for p_c < p < p_u there are infinitely many, and for p \ge p_u exactly one (Benjamini and Schramm [10]). No exact values are known, so no dashed line is drawn there.

At the critical point the clusters are random fractals. On the triangular lattice their crossing probabilities have an exact limit (Cardy's formula, proved by Smirnov [8]); on the square grid that is still open.
