About the model

Every cell holds one of q opinions (colors). Now and then a cell looks at its neighbors and changes its mind. In the classic voter model it simply copies a neighbor chosen at random, so popular opinions spread, but nobody is ever convinced by a majority.

Here you choose how strongly a share of neighbors pulls, by typing a function g: a cell takes each opinion with odds g(share of neighbors holding it). g(x) = x is the voter model. g(x) = x2 favors the majority, so the borders between opinions smooth out like soap films. Majority rule freezes quickly. With noise ε, a cell sometimes picks an opinion at random, so the opinions never settle.

On a finite domain without noise, one opinion eventually wins everywhere (consensus), but in the voter model this can take a very long time, and the borders stay rough the whole way.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. The opinions can also live on a ball of a hyperbolic tiling or of a tree: every cell within R steps of a start cell, drawn in the Poincaré disk or the half-plane (drag to move around the plane, or press Slide to slide the whole picture), or, for a tree, spread out in rings. There the stripes start becomes slices around the start. On the whole tree or plane, the voter model never reaches agreement: the opinions coexist forever.

Things to try: press Play with the voter rule, then switch to g(x) = xa and slide a from 1 to 3 and back. Try more opinions, the stripes start, a little noise, or the anti-voter rule.

The model. The domain is a graph G = (V, E): the cells, joined to their 4 or 8 neighbors. A state is \sigma \in \{1, \dots, q\}^V. For a cell v with d(v) neighbors, let x_c(v) = \#\{w \sim v : \sigma(w) = c\} / d(v). Each cell has a rate-1 exponential clock. When v's clock rings, its new opinion is

\mathbb{P}(c) = \frac{\varepsilon}{q} + (1 - \varepsilon) \frac{g(x_c(v))}{\sum_{c'} g(x_{c'}(v))},

and if every g(x_{c'}(v)) = 0, the second part keeps v's opinion. Negative values of g count as 0. The first of the |V| clocks rings after an \mathrm{Exp}(|V|) time and is a uniform cell, independent of when [1, Theorem 2.3.3], so the sim picks a uniform cell |V| times per unit of time.

Special cases. g(x) = x, \varepsilon = 0 is the voter model of Clifford and Sudbury [2] and Holley and Liggett [3]: copy a uniform neighbor. With two opinions, a rule that switches with probability f(\rho), where \rho is the share of neighbors that disagree and f(\rho) + f(1 - \rho) = 1, is this rule with g = f; that family is the generalized voter model of Dornic, Chaté, Chave and Hinrichsen [4]. g(x) = x^a is the nonlinear q-voter model [5], which favors the majority for a > 1. g(x) = 1 - x with two opinions is the anti-voter model [6]. \varepsilon > 0 is the noisy voter model [7].

Duality and clustering. The voter model is dual to coalescing random walks: trace a cell's opinion back in time, and it follows a random walk to the cell it was copied from [3; 8, Chapter V]. So on \mathbb{Z}^d it clusters (two cells far apart end up agreeing) exactly when two random walks meet, which is in dimensions 1 and 2 [3]. In dimension 2 the density of disagreeing pairs decays only like 1/\log t [9]: the borders never smooth out, they just slowly thin. For g(x) = x^a with a > 1 the borders instead shrink by curvature, like the low-temperature Ising model [4, 5].

Who wins. On a finite connected graph with g(x) = x and no noise, consensus comes with probability 1, and with two opinions

M = \sum_{v} d(v)\, \mathbf{1}\{\sigma(v) = 1\}

is a martingale, so opinion 1 wins with probability M(0) / \sum_v d(v): its share of the degrees, which on a torus is its share of the cells [10]. The check page tests this.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. Here G is the ball B_R = \{ v : d(o, v) \le R \} of a regular tiling \{p, q\} of the hyperbolic plane (1/p + 1/q < 1/2; neighbors share a side) or of the regular tree of degree d, with d(o, v) the number of steps from the start cell o, and only the edges inside the ball. On the whole tree or tiling, a simple random walk is transient and drifts off at a positive speed [11], so two walks meet with probability less than 1, and by the duality above the voter model does not cluster: started from a random state, it converges to a mix of opinions that never settles [8, Chapter V]. On the finite ball consensus still comes, and opinion 1 wins with its share of the degrees, which is not its share of the cells: the cells on the edge of the ball have fewer neighbors. The stripes start here splits the ball into q slices by the direction of each cell from o in the disk.
