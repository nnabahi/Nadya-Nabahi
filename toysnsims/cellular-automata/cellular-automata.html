About the model

A cellular automaton is a row (or grid) of cells, each in one of b states (colors). At every step, every cell looks at a few cells around it, its neighborhood, and all the cells change at once, by the same rule. Nothing is random (unless you make the edges random), and yet simple rules can make very complicated pictures.

The rule as a number. Read the neighborhood's states as a number written in base b, the leftmost cell first: with 2 states and the neighborhood {left, itself, right}, "1 0 0" is 4. Then the new state is digit 4 of the rule number written in base b, counting from the right, starting at 0. Rule 30 is 00011110 in base 2, and its digit 4 is 1. So every rule is one number.

In 1D, the picture shows the tape over time: each row is one step, the first at the top. Rule 30, started from one cell, grows a triangle that looks random inside. In 2D, the picture is the grid itself, changing as you watch. Conway's Game of Life is one such rule: a cell is born with exactly 3 live cells around it, and survives with 2 or 3.

The statistics look at the picture as a coloring: its clusters (connected pieces of one color), their sizes, and the interface (the neighbor pairs of different colors), as in the coloring sims.

Things to try: in 1D, rules 90, 110 and 184; the random start; more states. In 2D, the Game of Life from a random start, then click cells to draw your own shapes (pause first). Try the edges: fixed at 1, or random.

The model. The cells are \Lambda = \mathbb{Z}_L (a tape of length L) or \Lambda = \mathbb{Z}_W \times \mathbb{Z}_H (a grid), with states \sigma \in \{0, \dots, b-1\}^{\Lambda}. The neighborhood is a list of offsets N = (u_0, \dots, u_{k-1}), listed in 1D from left to right and in 2D from the bottom-left offset, row by row, to the top-right one; the offset 0 (the cell itself) may be in it, in its place. The configuration of cell x is

C(x) = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \sigma(x + u_i)\, b^{\,k-1-i} \in \{0, \dots, b^k - 1\},

and rule n, with 0 \le n < b^{\,b^k}, makes the next state

\sigma'(x) = \left\lfloor n / b^{\,C(x)} \right\rfloor \bmod b,

digit C(x) of n in base b, for every cell at once. This is Wolfram's numbering [1]: with b = 2 and N = (-1, 0, 1) it gives his 256 "elementary" rules. A rule number past b^{\,b^k} - 1 is used mod b^{\,b^k}.

Edges. Cells x + u_i off the tape or grid are read as: the cell they wrap around to (a ring or a torus), a fixed state k, or a uniform random state drawn afresh at every step and for every outside cell.

Famous rules. Rule 30 from one cell looks random, and its middle column was used as a random number generator [2]. Rule 90 from one cell draws Pascal's triangle mod 2, a Sierpiński triangle [3]. Rule 110 can carry out any computation [4]. Rule 184 is a simple traffic model: each 1 is a car that moves right when the cell ahead is empty [2, p. 269]. The Game of Life [5] is, here, the 2-state rule on the 3 × 3 block around a cell (the cell itself in the middle of the list) with digit C equal to 1 exactly when the 8 cells around hold 3 ones, or 2 ones and the cell itself is 1; its rule number has 140 digits. Life too can carry out any computation [6].

Repeats. With wrapping or fixed edges, there are only b^{|\Lambda|} states and each one decides the next, so by the pigeonhole principle the run comes back to an earlier state within b^{|\Lambda|} steps, and from then on goes round the same cycle forever. The page notices the first repeat: it keeps a 53-bit fingerprint (a hash) of every state, for up to 200,000 steps, and says the step where the cycle starts and its period p.

Statistics. A cluster is a connected piece of cells in one state, joined through the 4 cells sharing a side (or the 8 sharing a side or a corner). Its area is its number of cells, its perimeter the number of cell pairs side by side or one above the other with one cell in it and one in another state (the perimeters add up to twice the interface). The interface is the number of such neighbor pairs in different states, with the corner pairs too when clusters connect through 8. In 2D they are taken on the grid. In 1D the share of each state and the interface are taken on the current tape, and the clusters on the whole picture (the tape over time, as a coloring of a rectangle, whose left and right sides are not joined).
