About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

The model. Fix a tile T, a compact set in the plane (or in space) around 0; the tiles are its copies q + T. The floor is the segment 0 \le x \le W of the line y = 0 (in space, the square [0, W]^2 of the plane z = 0). Particles come one at a time. Each carries a tile straight down from high above, its center on the vertical line through a uniformly random place across, and stops the first time its tile touches the floor or a tile already there. It stays there. So a tile that brushes the side of another sticks to it, as on the Discrete Ballistic Deposition page, and the deposit has holes under its surface. With periodic sides the place is uniform on 0 \le x < W and the floor's ends are glued together; with walls it is uniform on the places where the tile fits between them. For disks this is ballistic deposition off the lattice.

Where it stops, exactly. With its center at c, the tile c + T touches or overlaps q + T exactly when c is in q + D, with the difference set D = T - T = \{s - t : s, t \in T\}. Coming down from high above, the center first reaches q + D at its top above the line, and the tile first reaches the floor when its lowest point does. So the tile falling at x stops at the height \max\bigl(-\min_{t \in T} t_y,\ \max_q\, (q_y + \operatorname{top}_D(x - q_x))\bigr), where \operatorname{top}_D(u) is the highest point of D above u. For a disk of radius r, D is the disk of radius 2r, so \operatorname{top}_D(u) = \sqrt{4r^2 - u^2}. Otherwise the page works with T as a polygon with 4096 corners (each the farthest point of T in its direction), and D as the polygon its edges and those of -T make (for a convex tile, merged in order of angle [5, Section 13.3]; otherwise the outer envelope of their reduced convolution [6]). Its top above each point is then exact. In space a ball of radius r has D the ball of radius 2r, and a box the box twice as wide each way, both exact. Only tiles whose D reaches the line can matter, so the page files the tiles in columns at least as wide as D, each column by height, and stops looking down a column once its tiles are too low to reach above the best stop so far.

What it is about. As on the Discrete page: the surface roughens, and its interface width w is expected to grow like t^{\beta}, with t the number of layers, until it saturates at a size that grows like W^{\alpha} (the Family–Vicsek scaling [2, 3]). Ballistic deposition is believed to be in the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang class [3, 4]: in 2D \beta = 1/3 and \alpha = 1/2, and simulations in 3D give about \beta = 1/4 and \alpha = 1/3 [1]. That comes from simulations and from the KPZ equation, not from a proof. The class is expected not to depend on details like the lattice or the tile's shape, so the same exponents should show here, and this page lets you test that. The theorems on the Discrete page are for the lattice; I don't know of any for this model.

The numbers. The height of the surface at a point is the top of the highest tile above it, measured at 8 evenly spaced points across each tile's width (in 3D, 3 by 3), over the places where a tile's center can be (with walls, not right next to them, where a round tile seldom reaches down). The mean height and the width are from those. The growth exponent is fitted as the slope of \log w against \log n by least squares, from n/10 to n.

Random numbers. Particle p falls at a place made from the seed and p. So it falls in the same place however many come after it, and going from n to n + 1 particles only adds a tile.
