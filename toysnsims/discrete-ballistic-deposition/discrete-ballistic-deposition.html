Falling rule

This lattice's blocks cut through cells at their sides, so walls wouldn't be straight: here the sides are periodic.

Every cell below height 0 is the floor (the gray layer is its top). Each particle falls straight down, at a uniformly random place across, and stops in the first cell it passes through that is next to the deposit or the floor; that cell joins the deposit. Down the edges, it starts on that line just above the deposit's reach, and steps from cell to cell, each time to a uniformly random neighbor lower down, until it is next to the deposit. On the square grid the two rules are the same. With periodic sides the strip's right edge is glued to its left (in 3D, its back to its front too). On the square grid a block is one cell.

View

Each cell is colored by when it joined: from blue (first) to red (just now). At a slow speed you can watch the latest particle fall down its dashed line; its cell shows when it lands. In 2D, click the picture to drop a particle there yourself.

Run

The same seed always gives the same deposit, and more particles only add cells: particle p falls at a place made from its own random numbers. Clicked particles stay in the run (Go to, the seed and the lattice keep them) and don't use up the random particles' numbers.

About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

The model [1]. On the square grid: a row of L columns, all of height h(i) = 0. Each particle picks a column i uniformly at random and falls straight down it, sticking to the first cell it touches, from above or from the side. So h(i) becomes \max\bigl(h(i - 1),\ h(i) + 1,\ h(i + 1)\bigr), with i \pm 1 taken around the strip (periodic sides) or left out past a wall. A particle that sticks to the side of a taller column leaves holes under it.

On any lattice. The same, with "falls down a column" made precise. A strip of the lattice, L repeating blocks wide (in 3D, L \times L), has a floor: every cell whose middle is below height 0. A particle falls down the vertical line through a uniformly random point across the strip, and stops in the first cell the line passes through that is next to the deposit or the floor (its neighbor: across a side, in 3D a face). On the square grid this is exactly the rule above. On other lattices wider cells are hit more often, in proportion to their width, which is what a uniform place means. Going down the line, the first such cell is always above every cell of the deposit on the line (the cell just above the deposit's highest one shares a side with it), so the page finds it as the highest empty cell on the line next to the deposit, exactly: each cell's pieces of the line come from its polygon (in 3D, its faces).

Down the edges. Another way to say "falls down a column" on any lattice: along the lattice's own edges. Each edge points from the middle of a cell to the middle of a neighbor, and it points down when its angle is strictly between 0 and -\pi, that is, its dot product with (0, -1) is positive (in 3D, with (0, 0, -1)). The particle starts in the cell the vertical line through its uniform point crosses lowest, in the first row of blocks too high to be next to the deposit, then steps down a uniformly random down edge, again and again, until its cell is next to the deposit or the floor, and stops there (a cell with no down edge would stop it too). On the square grid each cell has one down edge, to the cell below, so this is the rule above. Elsewhere the path wanders sideways, so this is a different model; the exponents above are not known for it.

What it is about. The surface roughens. Its interface width w (the standard deviation of the heights) is expected to grow like t^{\beta}, with t the number of layers (n / L), until it saturates at a size that grows like L^{\alpha}, after a time like L^{z} (the Family–Vicsek scaling [1, 2]). In 2D ballistic deposition is believed to be in the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) class [3], with \beta = 1/3, \alpha = 1/2 and z = 3/2; in 3D simulations give about \beta = 1/4 and \alpha = 1/3 [8]. That comes from simulations and from the KPZ equation, not from a proof for this model. Early on, before neighboring columns matter, the width grows like t^{1/2}, as for columns that grow on their own [2], so the fitted slope comes down toward 1/3 as the run goes on.

What is proved (on \mathbb{Z}^d, with a clock at each column instead of one particle at a time, which builds the same deposit). The height grows linearly, with a limit shape and a hydrodynamic limit [4]. In 2D the width grows at least like \log t [5]. The height's variance is at most C t in every dimension [6]. The surface seen from one column has a stationary law on the infinite lattice [7]. So the t^{1/3} law is still open.

The numbers. The height of the surface at a point is the top of the highest cell above it, measured at 8 evenly spaced points across each block (in 3D, 3 by 3); on the square grid that is the classic h(i). The mean height and the width are from those, the mean from the floor's own mean level. The growth exponent is fitted as the slope of \log w against \log n by least squares, from n/10 to n.

Random numbers. Particle p falls at a place made from the seed and p (and down the edges, picks each step from them too). So it falls in the same place however many come after it, and going from n to n + 1 particles only adds a cell. A particle you drop with a click doesn't count in p: the random particles after it fall where they would have anyway (onto a different deposit). The run keeps the clicks, at the moment each was made, so going back and forth with Go to, or changing the seed or the lattice, drops them again.
