About the model

The origins of this model are in studying bacterial colonies, growing outwards at their boundaries into the empty surroundings. To simplify, we cut up space into a grid and give each cell a 1 if it contains bacteria, or a 0 if it’s empty. The boundary is all the empty cells directly next to an occupied one. At each step, the colony expands by picking a boundary cell at random and filling it, adding it to the current cluster. This is the Eden model, introduced by Murray Eden in 1961, and it’s about as simple as growth gets. But don’t let its simplicity fool you! These basic rules create a model which is notoriously difficult to study.

There are two things to watch. First, the shape. Although the cluster grows at random, this randomness naturally averages out along the boundary. If it is allowed to grow for long enough, then the cluster forms into a limit shape, a round-ish blob. Surprisingly, nobody knows exactly what this shape is! Simulations suggest it’s not quite a circle, but no one has proved it yet. Secondly, the holes. Sometimes, the cluster wraps around an empty pocket, leaving it uncovered before coming back in later to fill it. Turn on Shade the holes to watch them come and go.

What if each cell counted the number of bacteria it held? Instead of a simple 0 or 1, each cell can pile up bacteria, until it reaches the Max Capacity. When in the 3D view, each block represents a bacterium. Once each cell can have more than 1 bacterium, the weight slider s comes in to bias where the next bacterium is born. A positive s favors crowded cells, leading to dense colonies; a negative s favors emptier ones, spreading the colony out faster; setting s back to 0 removes the bias.

What counts as a neighbor? Usually, two squares are neighbors if they share an edge, but many models allow diagonal neighbors as well. The Tile lets you build your own neighborhood: once a cell gains its first bacterium, those squares around the cell join the boundary. In 1973, Daniel Richardson proved a beautiful theorem: each custom tile pushes the cluster into its own limit shape! Draw your own and watch what shape it creates!

The rule. The tile T is a finite set of offsets in \mathbb{Z}^d (d = 1 or 2). The base and the available sites start as

D_0 = \{0\}, \qquad S_0 = \{0\} \cup T,

with one block on 0: H(0, 0) = 1. At each step n = 1, 2, \dots:

X_n \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(S_{n-1}), \qquad H(X_n, n) = H(X_n, n-1) + 1,
D_n = D_{n-1} \cup \{X_n\}, \qquad S_n = S_{n-1} \cup (X_n + T).

So S_n = \{0\} \cup (D_n + T), and after n steps there are n + 1 blocks. The uniform choice of X_n is the only randomness. In a domain \Omega, sites outside \Omega are never available; on a torus or a cycle the offsets wrap around.

nadya's models are presets: T = \{-1, +1\} is the two-sided mountain (randmountain.py, RandBlockStack.py), where X_n is uniform on the base interval or one site past either end; T = \{+1\} is the one-sided mountain (randonesidedmountain.py); and the 4 neighbors in 2D is the 2D mountain. For the two-sided mountain the base is an interval of L sites, and it grows at the next step with probability 2/(L+2).

The base is an Eden model. Given that the base grows at a step, the new site is uniform on S_{n-1} \setminus D_{n-1} = (D_{n-1} + T) \setminus D_{n-1}, the outer boundary of the base. So the base, watched only at the steps where it grows, is Eden's growth model [1] with the neighbors given by T; the mountain adds the heights on top.

Capacity. With a capacity M, the available sites are S_n = \{x \in \{0\} \cup (D_n + T) : H(x, n) < M\} (inside the domain), and the pick is uniform on S_n as before. With M = 1 every block lands outside the base, so |D_n| = n + 1 and the base is exactly Eden's growth model [1], step by step. On a bounded domain the run stops once every site is full.

Weight. With the weight s, the next site is no longer uniform on S_{n-1}:

\mathbb{P}(X_n = x) = \frac{(H(x, n-1) + 1)^s}{\sum_{y \in S_{n-1}} (H(y, n-1) + 1)^s}, \qquad x \in S_{n-1},

the number of blocks x will have, to the power s. At s = 0 this is the uniform rule above. An empty site has weight 1 for every s, so with M = 1 the weight changes nothing.

Random numbers: clocks. Every site x has, for each height h, its own exponential time E(x, h) of mean 1, all independent, made from the seed, x and h only. If x became available, or got its h-th block, at time t, its next block lands at t + E(x, h)/(h+1)^s, and the first block to land comes next. Exponential clocks racing each other ring first with chance proportional to their rates, and forget how long they have waited, so the sites picked have exactly the law of X_n above: it is the jump chain of a continuous-time Markov chain [7]. With M = 1 the landing times are Richardson's model [2], first passage percolation with exponential passage times. The E(x, h) don't depend on the tile, the domain, M or s, so with the same seed, moving any of them changes the mountain little by little, the way the p slider moves Bernoulli percolation. (The check page also runs the rule from a list of uniform numbers u_n \in [0, 1), picking site number \lfloor u_n |S_{n-1}| \rfloor as randmountain.py does, to compare with the Python block for block.)

Holes. In 2D, let K_n be the union of the closed unit squares of the base D_n. The holes are the bounded components of \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus K_n, so their number is the first Betti number \beta_1(K_n); as cells, a hole is a set of empty cells joined side to side (cells meeting only at a corner are apart, since K_n contains that corner). Cells outside the domain count as empty. A hole is born at the step that closes it off or splits an older hole, and dies at the step that fills its last cell; at a split, the piece that dies last keeps the old birth (the elder rule of persistent homology [8]). These are the holes studied for the Eden model in [9]. Only the base matters, not the heights. They are found exactly, backwards in time: take the base's cells away last-born first, joining each to its empty neighbors with a union–find; a cell with no empty neighbor is a one-cell hole dying at its step, and a cell joining several groups is a split, where every group but the one dying last was born at its step. The page redoes this as the run grows (not on a torus, where "bounded" means nothing, nor on graphs). With the clocks, an empty site's alarm runs at rate 1 for every M and s, so in clock time the base is Richardson's cluster [2] whatever they are: they change the step a hole appears at, not which holes appear.

Shape. For Eden's model (and Richardson's), the base at time t, scaled by 1/t, tends to a fixed convex set, the limit shape [2, 10], which is not known exactly. The page measures the base's shape directly. The convexity is \mathrm{area}(K_n)/\mathrm{area}(\mathrm{hull}(K_n)), with the convex hull kept up to date as the base grows: a new corner inside the hull changes nothing, and otherwise the hull of the old corners and the new one is found with Andrew's monotone chain [11]. The radius in direction \theta is the largest straight-line distance from the start to a base site (middle to middle) whose direction from the start lies in the 2^\circ slice around \theta; the charts divide it by its average over all directions, so they show the shape and not the size. Off the disk, a direction that stays above 1 over time is a bulge of the shape, and the rest is fluctuation.

On another tiling. In 2D, the Lattice menu (or a code in GomJau-Hogg notation) puts the mountain on any tiling of the plane by regular polygons, with its tiles or its corners as the cells, as in the other sims. There the cells are not all alike (a triangle pointing up is not a triangle pointing down moved over), so one list of offsets can't be everyone's tile. The tile is the ball T(x) = \{y \ne x : \mathrm{dist}(x, y) \le r\}, where \mathrm{dist} counts steps across sides, as on a graph (below); on the square grid r = 1 is the 4 neighbors. The rule is otherwise the same. Distances go from middle to middle, and K_n is the union of the closed polygons of the base, all in side lengths. The check page compares the tile's size with the coordination sequences of the square, triangular and hexagonal grids [12], and the holes with Euler's formula \beta_1 = 1 - V + E - F for a connected base [13]. The limit shape statements above are proved for the square grid [2, 10].

Penrose tilings. The menu also has Penrose's three tilings [15, 16]: pentagons, stars, boats and diamonds (P1), all with sides 1; kites and darts (P2), with sides 1 and \varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2; and thick and thin rhombs (P3), with sides 1. They never repeat: no shift moves one onto itself [14, Section 10.3]. So there is no torus, and the box is centered on the tiling's point of five-fold symmetry. On the whole plane the mountain starts at the cell nearest that point, and the page builds the tiling as the mountain reaches farther out, up to 160 side lengths from that point each way, and the mountain stops when it gets near there. The cells are the tiles, or for P2 and P3 the corners, and the tile is the ball T(x) as above. A pentagon of P1 in the notch of a star or a boat shares two sides with it, and is one step away. The times E(x, h) are made from where the cell x is, not from its number in the page's list, so with the same seed a box and the whole plane grow the same mountain until the one on the plane first leaves the box (the check page checks this).

On a graph. With "Hyperbolic plane or tree", the sites are the cells of a graph G: the tiling \{p, q\} of the hyperbolic plane by regular p-gons, q at each corner, which exists exactly when 1/p + 1/q < 1/2 [3] (cells are neighbors when they share a side), or the regular tree of degree d, drawn as the tiling by ideal d-gons. The tile is the ball T(x) = \{y \ne x : \mathrm{dist}_G(x, y) \le r\}, and the rule is the same: S_n = \{o\} \cup \bigcup_{x \in D_n} T(x). On the line and the square grid, r = 1 gives exactly the 1D and 2D mountains with T = \{-1, +1\} and the 4 neighbors (the check page grows both from the same u_n and compares them block for block).

Why the mountain stays low there. These graphs are non-amenable: there is a c > 0 with |\partial K| \ge c |K| for every finite set of cells K, where \partial K is the set of cells next to K but not in it [4]. With r = 1, the available sites are D_n \cup \partial D_n, so each step lands on a new site with probability |\partial D_n| / (|D_n| + |\partial D_n|) \ge c/(1+c), whatever happened before. So on average at least a fraction c/(1+c) of the steps widen the base, which grows in proportion to n, unlike on the line, where the base after n steps is only of order \sqrt{n} wide.

How the cells are made. The mountain's branches reach far from the start (the base is spread out like a tree), further than the computer's numbers can tell neighboring cells apart by their positions: at distance 30 two neighbors differ only in about the 14th digit. So the page builds the graph from its combinatorics instead. Every cell has a kind, read off from which of its neighbors are closer to the start, as close, or farther, and how the cells around its corners sit. A hyperbolic tiling has only finitely many such kinds (its "cone types" [5]), and a cell's kind decides its children's. The page learns the kinds from the cells near the start, where positions are precise, checks them against geometry 14 units out, and then builds every cell from them with whole numbers only. For a few large tilings (big p and q) there are too many cells near the start to learn from, and the page builds them by position, which works up to about distance 23; it says so under the graph's options.

The pictures. The Poincaré disk \{|z| < 1\} and the upper half-plane \{\operatorname{Im} w > 0\} are two pictures of the same plane: w = i(1+z)/(1-z) carries one onto the other, taking hyperbolic lengths to hyperbolic lengths [6]. The spread-out picture of a tree is the usual radial drawing: the cells at distance k from the start sit on the circle of radius k, and every cell's children share its slice of the circle equally.
