Tile T, centered at (0, 0, 0): the points (x, y, z) with a condition, or inside a surface F(x, y, z) = G(x, y, z)

Each ray from the center should leave T only once (a star-shaped tile), and for now a 3D tile must be convex. The tiles are copies of T, moved but never turned. The cluster starts as one tile at the center. Each particle carries a tile in from infinity by Brownian motion, and stops the moment its tile touches the cluster; that tile joins the cluster.

View

Each tile is colored by when it joined: from blue (first) to red (just now). The latest particle starts on the dashed launch circle and jumps from circle to circle, each a circle no tile reaches into, until it is close enough to touch; its tile shows when it lands. At a slow speed you can watch it walk. In 3D you see its path, where it is (a dark ball), and the see-through sphere it is jumping across.

Run

The same seed always gives the same cluster, and more particles only add tiles: particle p walks with its own random numbers.

Advanced

Stop within \varepsilon = tile radii

The jumps get smaller as the particle nears the cluster and never quite reach it, so it stops once it is this close (in units of the tile's radius) and then slides straight into contact. Smaller is more exact; it costs only a few more jumps (see All the details).

About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

The model. Fix a tile T, a compact set in the plane (or in space) around 0. The cluster starts as A_1 = \{q_1\} with q_1 = 0, standing for the tile q_1 + T. Given the centers A_n, a point B does Brownian motion from infinity, carrying the tile B_t + T with it, and stops at the first time \tau that tile touches a tile of the cluster. Then q_{n+1} = B_\tau and A_{n+1} = A_n \cup \{q_{n+1}\}. For disks this is the off-lattice DLA of Meakin [2], the continuous version of Witten and Sander's lattice model [1]; in space, balls.

A point instead of a tile. The tiles b + T and q + T meet exactly when b - q is in the difference set D = T - T = \{s - t : s, t \in T\}. So B is a point doing Brownian motion until it first reaches U = \bigcup_{q \in A_n} (q + D), and q_{n+1} has the harmonic measure from infinity of U [3, Chapter 3]. For a disk of radius r, D is the disk of radius 2r. The page works with T as a polygon with 4096 corners (each the farthest point of T in its direction) and finds the gap between two tiles exactly: for a disk directly, for a convex tile from the polygon D (built by merging the edges of T and -T in order of angle [6, Section 13.3]), and otherwise edge against edge, with a tree of circles around groups of edges to skip the far ones [7, Chapter 6].

In space, T is a polyhedron with 3458 corners, the farthest points of T in directions spread over the sphere (a grid of 24 by 24 on each face of a cube, spaced by equal angles). For a ball the gap is exact directly. For a convex tile, D is never built: the GJK algorithm [9; 7, Section 9.5] needs only the farthest point of D in a direction u, which is the farthest corner of T that way minus its farthest corner the other way. Each step gives an upper bound on the gap (a point of D) and a lower bound (a plane that all of D is behind), and the page uses the lower bound, so a jump is never too long. A tile that isn't convex isn't possible in 3D yet: the page checks convexity on the midpoints of many pairs of corners.

From infinity, exactly. All of U lies inside the circle of radius R = (the farthest center) + (the most D reaches). Brownian motion from infinity first meets that circle at a uniform point, by symmetry, so each particle starts there. A particle that later gets outside it, at x, is brought back to it the way Brownian motion from x comes back: at a point y with density \frac{|x|^2 - R^2}{2\pi R\,|x - y|^2}, the Poisson kernel for the outside of a disk [3, Chapter 3]. In space, Brownian motion from x ever comes back to the sphere only with chance R/|x| [3, Chapter 3], so "from infinity" means given that it reaches the cluster at all: a particle that comes back does so where Brownian motion would (the Poisson kernel for the outside of a ball, through inversion in the sphere), and one that doesn't is replaced by a new particle on the sphere, which gives exactly that law.

Walk on spheres [4]. Where B is, let r be its distance to U (or any smaller number). No point of U is inside the circle (sphere) of radius r around B, so B can't stop before it leaves that disk (ball), and Brownian motion leaves a disk at a uniform point of its circle. So B jumps straight there; by the strong Markov property this is exact. Near B the distance is the exact gap to each tile; farther out, a lower bound from a coarse grid of the tiles is enough.

The one approximation. The jumps get smaller as B nears U and never quite reach it, so B stops once it is within \varepsilon tile radii of U (the Advanced box), and then slides straight to the nearest point of U: its tile then just touches the cluster and overlaps no tile. As \varepsilon \to 0 this is exactly the model. Since U has a cone of a fixed opening at each point of its edge, the number of jumps grows only like \log(1/\varepsilon), in the plane and in space [5, Theorem 2 and Corollary 3].

What is known. Simulations of disks give a fractal dimension of about 1.71, and of balls about 2.5 [8], the same as the lattice model; no theorem says what it is. For the lattice model, Kesten proved a bound on how fast the radius can grow (see External Discrete DLA); I don't know of a proof of one for this model. The page fits the dimension from the radius of gyration R_g (the root mean square distance of the centers from their center of mass): the slope b of \log R_g against \log n by least squares, from n/10 to n, gives the estimate 1/b.

Random numbers. Move j of particle p uses a uniform number made from the seed, p and j. So particle p walks the same way however many come after it, and going from n to n + 1 particles only adds a tile.
