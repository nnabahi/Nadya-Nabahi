Options model check

Dimension

The cluster starts as the seed, one cell (in 2D, the outlined one). Each particle comes from far away and walks, every neighbor equally likely, until it first reaches a cell next to the cluster; that cell joins the cluster.

Launch

Radius of the launch circle × the reach

Walkers start on a circle (in 3D, a sphere) around the cluster. The reach is the farthest cell's distance plus one step. A walker that wanders 4 times farther out than the circle is brought back to it the way Brownian motion would come back, so it acts as if it came from infinity, except that near the circle a walk is not quite Brownian motion. The default, 2, is the smallest radius whose error a million walkers can't detect (see All the details); bigger is more exact and a little slower.

View

Each cell is colored by when it joined: from blue (first) to red (just now).

Run

The same seed always gives the same cluster, and more particles only add cells: particle p walks with its own random numbers.

About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

The model [1]. Fix a lattice (the square grid \mathbb{Z}^2 or any tiling of the plane, the cubic grid \mathbb{Z}^3 or any tiling of space) and a seed cell 0. Start with A(1) = \{0\}. Given the cluster A(n), let a particle do the simple random walk (each neighbor equally likely) from infinity, and let x_{n+1} be the first cell it reaches next to A(n) (an empty cell with a neighbor in it, an edge cell). Then A(n+1) = A(n) \cup \{x_{n+1}\}. So x_{n+1} has the harmonic measure from infinity of the edge E of A(n), H_E(y) = \lim_{|z| \to \infty} \mathbb{P}_z(S_T = y) with T the first time in E [2, Section 11; 3, Chapter 6]. In 3D the walk can wander off for good (it is transient), and "from infinity" means given that it reaches E.

What is known. The clusters are fractal: simulations give a dimension of about 1.71 in 2D and 2.5 in 3D [5], but no theorem says what it is. Kesten proved that, almost surely, the radius grows at most like n^{2/3} on \mathbb{Z}^2 [6] and at most like (n \log n)^{1/2} on \mathbb{Z}^3 [8, Theorem 1], so in the sense of the fitted dimension it is at least 3/2 in 2D and at least 2 in 3D. The page fits it from the radius of gyration R_g (the root mean square distance of the cells from their center): the slope b of \log R_g against \log n by least squares, from n/10 to n, gives the estimate 1/b.

From infinity: the one approximation. No program can start a walker at infinity. With the reach the farthest cell's distance plus one step (so every edge cell is inside it), each walker starts at the cell nearest a point taken uniformly on the circle (in 3D, the sphere) of radius R = launch \times reach. That is exactly where Brownian motion from infinity first meets the circle, by symmetry. A walker that gets farther than 4R, at a point x, is brought back to the circle the way Brownian motion from x comes back [4, Chapter 3]: at y with density proportional to |x - y|^{-d} on the circle or sphere (the Poisson kernel for the outside of a disk or ball; in 2D a wrapped Cauchy law for the angle); in 3D it comes back only with chance R/|x|, and otherwise is replaced by a new walker. Both steps would be exact for Brownian motion, and the walk looks like Brownian motion from far away, so the error shrinks as R grows.

Measuring the error. On \mathbb{Z}^2 the exact law of the next cell can be worked out: H_E is the one solution of \sum_{y \in E} a(x - y) H_E(y) = c for every x \in E and \sum_{y \in E} H_E(y) = 1, where a is the walk's potential kernel [2, Sections 12, 14 and 15]. From three clusters (three cells, 30 and 150 particles), a million walkers each landed where that law says at 2 reaches and at 3 (chi-square tests, all p > 0.17), while at 1 reach the 30-particle cluster's landings were off (p about 10^{-9}). So the default launch is 2. The model check page tests it again, and on the other lattices compares launches at 2 and 8 reaches.

Exact jumps. A walker far from every edge cell can't stop for a while. If the ball of cells at most r steps from it holds no edge cell, it can't reach the cluster before leaving that ball (it would have to pass an edge cell first), so the page jumps straight to the cell where it first leaves, drawn from the exact exit law of the ball (worked out once by solving the linear system for the walk's Green's function in the ball [3, Section 4.6]). By the strong Markov property [7, Theorem 1.4.2] the result is exactly the same as taking every step. How far the nearest edge cell is comes from the triangle inequality far out, and near the cluster from a search of the edge cells nearby. On a Penrose tiling, which never repeats, every step is taken.

Random numbers. Move j of particle p uses a uniform number made from the seed, p and j. So particle p walks the same way however many come after it, and going from n to n + 1 particles only adds a cell.
