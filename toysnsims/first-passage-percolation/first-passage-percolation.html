About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

The model [1, 2]. The domain is a graph G: the cells, each joined to the cells it shares a side with. Every edge e gets a passage time \tau_e \ge 0, all independent with the same law F, the law of w in the Random variables box. The passage time from x to y is the least total time of a path between them, and a path that takes exactly that long is a geodesic:

T(x, y) = \inf_{\gamma : x \to y} \sum_{e \in \gamma} \tau_e .

With the times on the cells instead, each cell v has its own \tau_v, and a path pays the time of every cell it steps onto (not the one it starts from). The ball B(t) = \{x : T(0, x) \le t\} is where a fluid poured in at the start 0 at time 0 has reached by time t, if it flows through each edge in its passage time; Hammersley and Welsh introduced the model as this picture of a fluid in a porous medium [1].

Eden growth. With exponential times of mean 1 on the cells, B(t) grows exactly like Eden's model [3]: the next cell to join is uniform among the cells next to the ball. A cell next to the ball has waited part of its time, and by the memoryless property of the exponential law, what is left of it is again exponential with mean 1, the same for each. With exponential times on the edges, the next cell is picked in proportion to its number of edges to the ball instead: Richardson's model [4]. The check page tests both.

The limit shape [5, 6; 2, Theorem 2.16]. On \mathbb{Z}^2, if F(0) < p_c = 1/2 (the critical point of bond percolation [7]) and \mathbb{E} \min(\tau_1, \dots, \tau_4)^2 < \infty for four independent times, then B(t)/t converges, almost surely, to a fixed compact convex set \mathcal{B} with the symmetries of the grid. Almost nothing more is known about \mathcal{B}. It isn't even known to be different from a disk for exponential times, though simulations say it is [8]. One thing is: if F has a smallest value r > 0 with F(\{r\}) more than the critical point of oriented percolation, then \mathcal{B} has flat edges [9, 10; 2, Theorem 2.24]. If instead F(0) \ge 1/2, the time constant is 0 and B(t)/t grows without bound [6, Theorem 6.1; 2, Theorem 2.5]. The law w ~ geometric(1/2) (on 0, 1, 2, …) has F(0) = 1/2 exactly: it sits at the critical point, where the zero-time edges form critical percolation clusters. The charts "Radius by direction" show the ball's shape against a disk, and "T(0, x)/|x|" shows the times settling down to the time constant in each direction.

Fluctuations [2, Section 3]. In d = 2 the fluctuations of T(0, n e_1) are believed to be of order n^{1/3}, as the KPZ scaling theory predicts. That is proved only for exactly solvable last passage models [11]; for first passage percolation the best bound known is \mathrm{Var}\, T(0, n e_1) \le C n / \log n, proved by Benjamini, Kalai and Schramm for times taking two values [12], and then for a wide class of laws [13; 2, Section 3.2].

Geodesics [2, Section 4]. With a law that has no point masses, the geodesic between two cells is unique (almost surely); with point masses there can be several, and the sim draws one with the fewest steps. Geodesics heading the same way from far apart tend to merge: Licea and Newman showed it for almost every direction, under a curvature assumption on \mathcal{B} [14, 15], and Damron and Hanson a form of it under much weaker assumptions [16]. Under general conditions there are, almost surely, at least 4 different infinite geodesics from the start (one-sided: they start there and go off to infinity) [17]. Whether there is a geodesic infinite in both directions is open; Damron and Hanson rule them out in fixed directions, under assumptions [18]. A geodesic of length n is believed to stray about n^{2/3} from the straight line; the relation \chi = 2\xi - 1 between this exponent and the fluctuations' is proved under assumptions by Chatterjee [19]. Pairs mode shows the merging: where geodesics share edges, they run side by side in a bundle.

Holes. A hole of B(t) is a bounded piece of the plane outside it, a pocket the fluid has gone around. It is born when the ball closes around it, and dies when its last cell is reached. When a hole is split in two, the piece that is filled last keeps the old birth (the "elder rule" of persistent homology [20]). Holes are counted on flat domains, not on a torus or the hyperbolic plane.

The Random variables box. The passage time is the random variable w. Its line can give it a law from the list, like w ~ exp(1), or a density typed in x, Desmos style, where a piece whose condition is an equation is a point mass: for example w ~ {x = 0: p_0, x = 3: p_3, x >= 5: (1 - p_0 - p_3) e^(-x + 5), 0} puts mass p_0 at 0 and p_3 at 3, and spreads the rest over [5, \infty) (it must all add up to 1). Or w can be a formula of other random variables, like w = min(a, b) with a ~ exp(1) and b ~ unif(0, 2), worked out anew on each edge (the help page has every law and how to type them). Each edge's time is \tau = F^{-1}(U), the least x with F(x) \ge U, for a uniform U [21, Section 1.2]. Each edge keeps its own U, from the seed and where the edge is, so moving a slider moves every time a little, all the same way, and the ball and the geodesics change smoothly. Times can't be negative: a law for w must be 0 below 0, and a formula that comes out negative on an edge is refused.

How it is computed. Dijkstra's algorithm [22] finds the cells in order of T(0, x), the nearest first, one at a time; each step of the run is one more cell, so watching it is watching B(t) grow, and t is the time of the last cell found. Cells with the same T are found in order of the fewest steps on a path that takes T, as if every edge took a tiny extra time. That matters when many times are 0: on the square grid with F(0) > 1/2, the edges with time 0 have an infinite cluster [7], so B(0) can be infinite, and the ball shown at t = 0 is then every cell within k steps of the start along edges with time 0, for a growing k. In Pairs mode, each pair's geodesic is found by Dijkstra's algorithm from one particle, stopped when it reaches the other. The whole plane is a box around the start that grows: a path that leaves the box crosses a cell on its edge first, so the box gives the plane's T for every cell found before the first one on its edge. Then the box doubles, and every edge keeps its time. In Pairs mode on the whole plane, the particles are uniform in the middle of the first box.

Other lattices. The Lattice menu puts the cells on a tiling of the plane by regular polygons (its tiles or its corners), or on one of Penrose's tilings; on the hyperbolic plane or a tree, the domain is the ball of every cell within R steps of the start. There |x| is the number of steps from the start, not a length.
