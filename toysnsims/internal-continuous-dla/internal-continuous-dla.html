Tile T, centered at (0, 0, 0): the points (x, y, z) with a condition, or inside a surface F(x, y, z) = G(x, y, z)

Each ray from the center should leave T only once (a star-shaped tile), and for now the tile must be a ball, or a cube with its sides along x, y and z. A tile with dents, like the star, is slower. The tiles are copies of T, moved but never turned. Each particle carries a tile out from the source, the first tile's center, by Brownian motion, and stops the moment its tile overlaps no tile of the cluster; that tile joins the cluster. The first particle stops at once.

View

Each tile is colored by when it joined: from blue (first) to red (just now). The latest particle starts at the source and jumps from circle to circle, each a circle where its tile would overlap the cluster everywhere, until it is close enough to a place where its tile fits; its tile shows when it lands. At a slow speed you can watch it walk. In 3D the tiles are see-through while it walks, and you see its path, where it is (a dark ball), and the see-through sphere it is jumping across.

Run

The same seed always gives the same cluster, and more particles only add tiles: particle p walks with its own random numbers.

Advanced

Stop within \varepsilon = tile radii

The jumps get smaller as the particle nears a place where its tile fits, and never quite reach it, so it stops once it is this close (in units of the tile's radius) and moves straight there. Smaller is more exact; it costs only a few more jumps (see All the details).

About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

The model. Fix a tile T, a compact set in the plane (or in space) around 0. The cluster starts empty, A_0 = \emptyset. Given the centers A_n, a point B does Brownian motion from the source 0, carrying the tile B_t + T with it, and stops at the first time \tau that tile overlaps no tile q + T, q \in A_n (their insides are apart; touching is allowed). Then q_{n+1} = B_\tau and A_{n+1} = A_n \cup \{q_{n+1}\}. The first particle stops at once, so q_1 = 0. The tiles always touch and never overlap, and the cluster of n particles has n tiles. It is a continuous version of internal DLA on a lattice [1] (see Internal Discrete DLA).

A point instead of a tile. The tiles b + T and q + T overlap exactly when b - q is inside the difference set D = T - T = \{s - t : s, t \in T\}. So \tau is the first time B leaves U = \bigcup_{q \in A_n} (q + D^\circ), the union of the insides, and q_{n+1} has the harmonic measure of U from 0: where Brownian motion from 0 first leaves it [3, Chapter 3]. For a disk of radius r, D is the disk of radius 2r. A point outside U is a free point: a place where a tile fits. A hole inside the cluster where a whole tile fits is free too, so holes fill up. The page works with T as a polygon with 4096 corners (each the farthest point of T in its direction); for a convex tile, D is the polygon made by merging the edges of T and -T in order of angle [6, Section 13.3]. For a tile with dents, D is still star-shaped around 0 (if s - t is in it, so is \lambda s - \lambda t for 0 \le \lambda \le 1), and its edge is made of pieces of the reduced convolution of T and -T: the edges of each moved by the corners of the other that stick out, where the edge's direction is between those of the corner's two edges [7, 8]. So on each ray from 0, D's outline is the farthest of those segments. A disk, and in space a ball, are used exactly. In space a cube is recognized from its polyhedron (its corners all on the cube's faces, and the cube's 8 corners among them).

Walk on spheres [4]. Where B is, let r be its distance to the nearest free point (or any smaller number). U holds the disk (ball) of radius r around B, so B can't stop before it leaves that disk, and Brownian motion leaves a disk at a uniform point of its circle. So B jumps straight there; by the strong Markov property this is exact. For r the page takes the larger of two lower bounds: how deep B is in the copy q + D it is deepest in, and, deep inside the cluster, the radius of a ball around the source that U is known to hold, minus how far B is from the source. That ball comes from a grid of cells looked at in order of distance from the source: a cell counts once all of it is inside U, which is checked in one copy, or in quarters, and at the smallest quarters with the exact nearest free point (next).

The nearest free point, exactly. Where two copies overlap only a little, B can be close to the edges of both and still far from every free point, so a small depth never stops B by itself. Instead the page finds the free point p nearest B exactly. It is on the edge of U, which is made of pieces of the copies' edges. On a circle, a sphere or a line, the distance to B stops changing at only two places, the nearest point and the farthest. So p is a copy's edge point nearest B, a point where two copies' edges cross, or in space the point nearest B of a circle where two spheres meet, or a point where three meet. The page lists those near B, keeps the free ones, and takes the nearest. For cubes there are even fewer to try: each coordinate of p is B's own or a face's.

The one approximation. The jumps get smaller as B nears a free point and never quite reach it, so B stops once the nearest free point is within \varepsilon tile radii (the Advanced box), and moves straight to it: its tile then just touches the cluster and overlaps no tile. If no free point is within \varepsilon, B jumps that far. As \varepsilon \to 0 this is exactly the model. For a given cluster, the free points have a cone of some fixed opening at every point of the edge of U (where two copies' edges cross, the angle between them; it closes up only where two round copies just touch, which has chance 0), so the number of jumps grows only like \log(1/\varepsilon) [5, Theorem 2 and Corollary 3]; the model check measures it.

What is known. On a lattice, internal DLA grows into a ball, with errors far smaller than its radius [2] (see Internal Discrete DLA). I don't know of a theorem about the shape of this continuous version, with tiles that only touch, so its shape is an open question here.

Random numbers. Move j of particle p uses a uniform number made from the seed, p and j. So particle p walks the same way however many come after it, and going from n to n + 1 particles only adds a tile.
