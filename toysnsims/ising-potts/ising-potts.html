About the model

Every cell has one of q colors. Neighbors like to agree: a coloring is more likely the more neighbor pairs share a color, and how much more is set by β, one over the temperature. With q = 2 colors this is the Ising model of a magnet; with more colors it is the Potts model.

When β is small (hot), the colors are a fine random speckle. When β is big (cold), one color takes over most of the domain. In between, at the critical point βc, there are blobs of every size, inside blobs, inside blobs. On the square grid βc = log(1 + √q).

The sim runs a random process that settles into this distribution. The heat bath picks a random cell and recolors it, with the odds of each color set by its neighbors. Swendsen–Wang joins agreeing neighbors into random clusters and recolors whole clusters at once, which is much faster near the critical point.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. The model also runs on a ball of a hyperbolic tiling or of a tree: every cell within R steps of a start cell. It is drawn in the Poincaré disk or the half-plane (drag to move around the plane, or press Slide to slide the whole picture), or, for a tree, spread out in rings, and all three zoom. Most of a ball's cells are on its edge, so cooling it down looks quite different from the square grid.

Things to try: press Play at βc and watch the blobs grow; slide β up to freeze the picture or down to melt it. Push h a little to tilt the race toward color 1. Try q = 3 and q = 5: from q = 5 on, the change at βc becomes a sudden jump instead of a gradual one.

The model. The domain is a graph G = (V, E): the cells, joined to their 4 or 8 neighbors (on other lattices, see below). A coloring is \sigma \in \{1, \dots, q\}^V, and the Potts model [1] gives it probability

\pi(\sigma) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\Big( \beta \sum_{\{u,v\} \in E} \mathbf{1}\{\sigma(u) = \sigma(v)\} + \beta h \sum_{v \in V} \mathbf{1}\{\sigma(v) = 1\} \Big),

with Z the sum of the same over all colorings. On a box the cells on the edge simply have fewer neighbors (“free boundary”). For q = 2, write s(v) = +1 for color 1 and -1 for color 2. Then \mathbf{1}\{\sigma(u) = \sigma(v)\} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + s(u)s(v)), so this is the Ising model [2] at inverse temperature \beta/2 and field h/2.

Heat bath. Pick v uniformly and draw its new color from \pi given the others:

\mathbb{P}(\sigma(v) = k \mid \text{rest}) \propto \exp\big(\beta\, n_k(v) + \beta h\, \mathbf{1}\{k = 1\}\big),

where n_k(v) is the number of neighbors of v with color k. This is Glauber dynamics; it is reversible with respect to \pi, and on a connected graph it converges to \pi [3; 4, Section 3.3]. A sweep is |V| updates.

Swendsen–Wang. Open each edge whose ends agree with probability p = 1 - e^{-\beta}; then give each cluster C of open edges a new color, color 1 with weight e^{\beta h |C|} and each other color with weight 1 [5]. This leaves \pi unchanged, because \pi is a marginal of a joint law of colorings and edges (the Edwards–Sokal coupling [6]) whose edge marginal, for h = 0, is the Fortuin–Kasteleyn random cluster model with parameters (p, q) [7].

The phase transition. On the square grid with 4 neighbors and h = 0, the critical point is

\beta_c(q) = \log(1 + \sqrt{q})

for every q \ge 1, the self-dual point of the random cluster model (Beffara and Duminil-Copin [8]); for q = 2 this is Onsager's \tfrac{1}{2}\log(1 + \sqrt{2}) in the Ising convention [9]. The transition is continuous for q \le 4 [10] and discontinuous for q > 4 [11]: one color suddenly takes over a positive share of the domain. With 8 neighbors no exact \beta_c is known.

Exact numbers for q = 2. Above the critical point, the spontaneous magnetization of the infinite grid is (Yang [12], in this page's \beta)

m^*(\beta) = \big(1 - \sinh(\beta)^{-4}\big)^{1/8},

and at \beta_c the share of agreeing neighbor pairs is \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + 1/\sqrt{2}) \approx 0.8536 [9]. The check page compares the sim with both.

Other lattices. The Lattice menu puts the cells on a tiling of the plane by regular polygons with sides of length 1 that repeats in two directions: the 11 where every corner looks the same, the 20 with two kinds of corner [17, Sections 2.1 and 2.2], or any tiling typed as a code in GomJau-Hogg notation [18] (see how codes work). The cells are either the tiles, neighbors when they share a side, or the corners, neighbors when a side joins them. These two graphs are dual to each other: the tiles of triangles and the corners of hexagons make the same graph (the honeycomb), and so do the tiles of hexagons and the corners of triangles (the triangular lattice). A box is the cells whose middles (or corners) lie in a rectangle, its sides in side lengths, and a torus is a whole number of the tiling's repeating blocks each way.

Penrose tilings. The menu also has Penrose's three tilings [25, 26]: pentagons, stars, boats and diamonds (P1), all with sides 1; kites and darts (P2), with sides 1 and \varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2; and thick and thin rhombs (P3), with sides 1. They never repeat: no shift moves one onto itself [17, Section 10.3]. So there is no torus, and the box is centered on the tiling's point of five-fold symmetry. Only P1's tiles can be cells, not its corners: the inner corner of a star or a boat touches just two tiles. P2 and P3 are built as in [17, Section 10.3]: each tile is cut in half into two kinds of triangle, and each triangle is split again and again into smaller ones of the same two kinds. P1 is built as Penrose found it [26]: each pentagon is split into six smaller ones, and the gaps are filled with smaller stars, boats, diamonds and pentagons, again and again. Every corner is worked out exactly, as a whole-number combination of the five directions of a regular pentagon. The model check checks that every corner is one of the kinds a Penrose tiling allows (Conway's seven for kites and darts [28], de Bruijn's eight for rhombs [27], and angles adding up to 360° for P1); that the number of kites per dart, and of thick rhombs per thin one, is close to \varphi, as it is in the limit [17, Section 10.3]; and that each of P1's six kinds of tile (three kinds of pentagon, the star, the boat and the diamond) makes up close to the share Kociemba computes [29].

On the triangular lattice and the honeycomb the critical point is known exactly for every q \ge 1. With v = e^{\beta} - 1, it is the positive root of

v^3 + 3v^2 = q \ \text{(triangular lattice)}, \qquad v^3 = 3qv + q^2 \ \text{(honeycomb)}

(Baxter, Temperley and Ashley [19]; the proof of [8] covers both lattices too). The two lattices are dual to each other, and the two roots multiply to q, as the duality of the random cluster model says they must [20, Chapter 6]; the square grid is dual to itself, and there v = \sqrt{q}. For q = 2 these are \beta_c = \tfrac{1}{2}\log 3 on the triangular lattice and \log(2 + \sqrt{3}) on the honeycomb, found by Houtappel [21] and Wannier [22] (in the Ising convention, half of these). On the corners of the kagome tiling (3.6.3.6), the Ising model has \beta_c = \tfrac{1}{2}\log(3 + 2\sqrt{3}) [23], so there the page offers a \beta_c button for q = 2 only. For the Ising model, the critical point of any of these tilings can be worked out exactly from one repeating block [24], but this page doesn't do that yet, so on the other tilings it offers no \beta_c button.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. Here G is the ball B_R = \{ v : d(o, v) \le R \} of a regular tiling \{p, q\} of the hyperbolic plane (1/p + 1/q < 1/2; neighbors share a side) or of the regular tree of degree d, with d(o, v) the number of steps from the start cell o, and only the edges inside the ball (free boundary, as on a box). On the whole tree of degree d, the Ising model (q = 2, h = 0) has more than one Gibbs measure exactly when (d - 1) \tanh(\beta/2) > 1 [13], and the one with free boundary stays extremal until (d - 1) \tanh^2(\beta/2) > 1 [14]. On a ball, though, the heat bath has relaxation time polynomial in the number of cells for every fixed \beta, on trees and on hyperbolic tilings alike [15]. The Ising model on hyperbolic tilings also has a phase transition, at a \beta_c not known exactly [16], so this page offers no \beta_c button there.
