About the model

Start with the vector (1, 0). At every step, pick one of the matrices at random and multiply the vector by it. Do this N times side by side, and draw a dot where each run's vector is: that's the cloud. The blue dots do the same to (0, 1), with the same picks.

Matrices stretch and turn, so a long product usually grows (or shrinks) like e^{\gamma t} for one fixed number \gamma, the growth rate, even though every run picks differently.

When every column of every matrix adds up to 1 (your D12 and D13), the dots stay on the line x + y = 1, and settle into a fixed random pattern. For D13 that pattern is a fractal "dust".

Things to try: press Play on D13 and watch the dust appear, then switch to the Histogram; change a number in a matrix and watch the cloud bend; type a letter like a into an entry to get a slider; give one matrix weight 3; try randmatAB.py with the scale "divide by ρt" or "length 1".

The product. The matrices M_1, \dots, M_k have weights w_1, \dots, w_k. The steps X_1, X_2, \dots are independent, with X_s = M_i with probability w_i / (w_1 + \dots + w_k), and

v_t = X_t v_{t-1} = P_t v_0, \qquad P_t = X_t X_{t-1} \cdots X_1, \qquad v_0 = (1, 0).

The red dots are P_t (1, 0) and the blue dots P_t (0, 1), the two columns of P_t. An entry can use the random variables of the Random variables box: each step gets new, independent copies of every name, one number per name in all the entries of the step. Your D12 is the one matrix \begin{pmatrix} u_1 & u_2 \\ 1 - u_1 & 1 - u_2 \end{pmatrix} with u_1, u_2 \sim \mathrm{unif}(0, 1). The average matrix E[X] in the Statistics averages such entries numerically: on a fine grid of the uniform numbers behind them when they use at most four names, which is exact for entries linear in each of those numbers (like D12's), and otherwise over many draws.

Growth rate. If the average of \log^+ |X_1| is finite (true here, since every entry is bounded), then with probability 1

\frac{1}{t} \log |P_t| \to \gamma,

a fixed number, the top Lyapunov exponent [1]. Any matrix norm gives the same limit; the sim uses the largest entry in size. The average satisfies E[v_t] = E[X]^t v_0, because the steps are independent, but \gamma is usually not \log \rho(E[X]): compare the two in the Statistics.

Stochastic matrices. If every column adds up to 1, so does v_t, so v_t = (x_t, 1 - x_t) and each matrix acts on x as a map of the line. For D13, A: x \mapsto 0.8 + 0.1x and B: x \mapsto 0.3 + 0.3x, two contractions with fixed points 8/9 and 3/7. The law of x_t converges to the unique stationary law of this random map, even though each run's x_t keeps jumping; the reversed product X_1 \cdots X_t v_0 converges for every run [2]. That law lives on the attractor of the two maps [3]. The images of [3/7, 8/9] under A and B don't overlap, so the attractor is a Cantor set, of dimension s solving 0.1^s + 0.3^s = 1, about 0.41 [3].

Long products. Each run stores P_t = e^{L_t} Q_t, where the largest entry of Q_t is 1 in size, so products far past the largest computer number (about e^{709}) are still exact up to rounding. The scale "divide by \rho^t" is the scaling of randmatcltmultraj.py.

Random numbers. Step s of run n picks its matrix with a uniform number c fixed by the seed, n and s (matrix i when c falls in its interval of length equal to its chance). Each random variable's copy there is F^{-1}(U), where F is its law and U a uniform number fixed by the seed, the name, n and s, so it has the law F [4, Theorem 1.2.2]. Changing a matrix, a weight or a law keeps every uniform number, so the cloud moves continuously (how the random variables work).
