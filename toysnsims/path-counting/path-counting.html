About the model

Start at a point (x, y) of the grid and walk to (0, 0), one step left or one step down at a time. Some steps are open and some are closed, by a rule you type: X(x, y) = 1 opens the step left from (x, y), and Y(x, y) = 1 the step down. The number f(x, y) counts the ways to get home using only open steps. The picture colors every point by it, from blue (few) to red (many).

The numbers grow very fast: with every step open, there are already about 10^{35} ways home from (60, 60). So the picture looks at one line at a time, at right angles to a direction you pick with the angle t, and divides by the largest number on it. Turn on "Show the line": the red dots (balls in 3D) are its points, as high as their share of the largest. With every step open and the angle at 45°, the line is x + y = k and they make a bell curve.

Things to try: "All edges open" at 45°, then slide c and watch the bell get wider. Your periodic rule, where the step down from row y is open only above every second or third point; slide p and turn the angle to find where its curve looks most like a bell. Switch to 3D and turn the picture around: the whole surface shows every line's curve at once, a ridge running out from the corner.

Weights. X and Y can be any numbers 0 or above, not just 0 and 1. Then f adds up, over the paths, the product of the weights along each path. It is no longer a count of paths (the gray line under the picture says so), but the same picture still works.

The rule. On the grid \{0, \dots, N\}^2,

f(0, 0) = 1, \qquad f(x, y) = X(x, y)\, f(x-1, y) + Y(x, y)\, f(x, y-1),

where the first term is left out when x = 0 and the second when y = 0. Unrolling the recursion,

f(x, y) = \sum_{\pi : (x, y) \to (0, 0)} \ \prod_{e \in \pi} w(e),

summing over the paths \pi with unit steps left and down, where w(e) is X at the right end of a horizontal step and Y at the top end of a vertical one. With weights in \{0, 1\}, f(x, y) is the number of paths through open edges. A weight that is undefined (like \{x > 2: 1\} at x = 1) or below 0 counts as 0.

All edges open. Then f(x, y) = \binom{x+y}{x}, the number of lattice paths [1]. On the line x + y = c, f(x, c - x) = \binom{c}{x} = 2^c\, P(S_c = x) with S_c \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(c, 1/2), and the de Moivre–Laplace theorem [2] says that, centered at c/2 and scaled by \sqrt{c}/2, this tends to the standard Gaussian as c \to \infty:

\binom{c}{x} \Big/ \binom{c}{\lfloor c/2 \rfloor} \approx \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x - c/2)^2}{c/2}\Big).

On another line x \cos t + y \sin t = c with 0 < t < \pi/2, Stirling's formula [3] gives \log \binom{x+y}{x} \approx (x+y)\, H\!\big(\tfrac{x}{x+y}\big) with H(t) = -t \log t - (1-t)\log(1-t). Along the line this is c times a smooth function of x/c with a single highest point inside, so expanding it to second order there (Laplace's method [4]) gives a bell curve again, of width of order \sqrt{c}.

Your periodic rule (the old site's default): for each row y, d_y = 3 with probability p and 2 otherwise, independently, and the step down from (x, y) is open only when d_y divides x:

X = 1, \qquad Y(x, y) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } d_y \mid x \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}

A heuristic, not a theorem: a path needs on average about 2 + p horizontal steps per vertical one to line up with the open columns, which suggests the most bell-like line moves from x + y = c toward x + (2 + p) y = c (the angle t = \arctan(2 + p)) as p goes from 0 to 1. Nothing here proves a Gaussian limit for this rule.

Other weights, and last passage percolation. With weights X, Y \ge 0, f is the partition function of a directed polymer [5]: writing w(e) = e^{\beta \omega(e)}, f = \sum_\pi e^{\beta\, \omega(\pi)}, where \omega(\pi) is the total of the \omega's along \pi. Since one path is the heaviest and there are at most \binom{x+y}{x} paths,

\max_\pi \omega(\pi) \;\le\; \tfrac{1}{\beta} \log f(x, y) \;\le\; \max_\pi \omega(\pi) + \tfrac{1}{\beta}\log \tbinom{x+y}{x},

so as \beta \to \infty, \frac{1}{\beta}\log f tends to the last passage time \max_\pi \omega(\pi) [6]: the same recursion with (\max, +) in place of (+, \times).

The lines. Grid points rarely lie exactly on x \cos t + y \sin t = c, so the page takes the nearest ones. With w = \max(\cos t, \sin t), every grid point is on exactly one line number

k = \operatorname{round}\big((x \cos t + y \sin t)/w\big),

which is one point per column when t \ge \pi/4 (one per row otherwise), and line k is the one at c = k w. At t = \pi/4 it is exactly x + y = k.

Normalizing. "Normalized" divides each f(x, y) by the largest f on its own line k, so every line tops out at 1. In 3D each square of the grid is cut into two triangles, each flat, with its color fading linearly between its corners.

How it is computed. Row by row from the bottom, in \log f (with \log(e^s + e^t) worked out without forming e^s), so nothing overflows. The statistics treat f along the line, divided by its sum, as chances for the position x (or y for a line with one point per row); the Gaussian has their mean and standard deviation. Each random() mixes the seed, the formula, where it is worked out and which random() it is with the hash function cyrb53 [7], so it is the same number every time it is worked out again.
