About the model

Blocks are dropped one at a time and pile up into a mountain. The base is the set of sites that already have a block. A block may land on any site of the base, or on a site the tile reaches from the base. It picks one of these available sites completely at random, all equally likely.

With the tile {−1, +1} on a line, a block lands on the base or one site past either end, so the base only grows when a block lands just past an end. Most blocks land on the base, and the mountain gets taller much faster than it gets wider.

Things to try: click an available site to drop the next block there yourself; the one-sided tile {+1}; a tile with a gap, like {−2, +1}; 2D with 4 or 8 neighbors; a tile of your own, clicked in the grid; a thin ring, which spreads more than it stacks; or a small domain, where the base soon fills it and the mountain only gets taller. In 2D, zoom with the mouse wheel, a pinch, or the + and − buttons, and drag the picture to look around; on a torus, zoomed out, it shows several times side by side. Or switch the 2D view to 3D, to see each site's blocks as a stack of cubes or coins, and turn the mountain around. The button in the picture's top left corner shows it on the whole screen.

Hyperbolic plane or tree. The same mountain on a tiling of the hyperbolic plane, or on a tree, drawn in a disk where the cells shrink toward the edge (the edge is infinitely far away). Drag to travel across the plane (or press Slide to slide the whole picture), and zoom as in 2D to see the small cells near the edge. There, every patch of cells has a long edge compared to its size, so the base spreads fast and the mountain stays low: compare {7, 3} with the 2D grid. The whole tiling (or tree) is drawn in thin gray underneath, in 3D too; on a tree, each site is a coin on its vertex. Besides the disk, the plane can be drawn as the half-plane above a line, and a tree can be spread out, with the start in the middle and each step away on the next ring (drag it and zoom it as in 2D).

The rule. The tile T is a finite set of offsets in \mathbb{Z}^d (d = 1 or 2). The base and the available sites start as

D_0 = \{0\}, \qquad S_0 = \{0\} \cup T,

with one block on 0: H(0, 0) = 1. At each step n = 1, 2, \dots:

X_n \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(S_{n-1}), \qquad H(X_n, n) = H(X_n, n-1) + 1,
D_n = D_{n-1} \cup \{X_n\}, \qquad S_n = S_{n-1} \cup (X_n + T).

So S_n = \{0\} \cup (D_n + T), and after n steps there are n + 1 blocks. The uniform choice of X_n is the only randomness. In a domain \Omega, sites outside \Omega are never available; on a torus or a cycle the offsets wrap around.

nadya's models are presets: T = \{-1, +1\} is the two-sided mountain (randmountain.py, RandBlockStack.py), where X_n is uniform on the base interval or one site past either end; T = \{+1\} is the one-sided mountain (randonesidedmountain.py); and the 4 neighbors in 2D is the 2D mountain. For the two-sided mountain the base is an interval of L sites, and it grows at the next step with probability 2/(L+2).

The base is an Eden model. Given that the base grows at a step, the new site is uniform on S_{n-1} \setminus D_{n-1} = (D_{n-1} + T) \setminus D_{n-1}, the outer boundary of the base. So the base, watched only at the steps where it grows, is Eden's growth model [1] with the neighbors given by T; the mountain adds the heights on top.

Random numbers. Step n uses a uniform number u_n \in [0, 1) and picks site number \lfloor u_n |S_{n-1}| \rfloor, as in randmountain.py. In 1D the sites are counted from left to right, as in the Python, so the same u's give exactly the same mountain; in 2D they are counted in the order they became available. The u_n come from the seeded generator seedrandom [2], and the same seed gives the same u_n whatever the tile and domain.

On a graph. With "Hyperbolic plane or tree", the sites are the cells of a graph G: the tiling \{p, q\} of the hyperbolic plane by regular p-gons, q at each corner, which exists exactly when 1/p + 1/q < 1/2 [3] (cells are neighbors when they share a side), or the regular tree of degree d, drawn as the tiling by ideal d-gons. The tile is the ball T(x) = \{y \ne x : \mathrm{dist}_G(x, y) \le r\}, and the rule is the same: S_n = \{o\} \cup \bigcup_{x \in D_n} T(x). On the line and the square grid, r = 1 gives exactly the 1D and 2D mountains with T = \{-1, +1\} and the 4 neighbors (the check page grows both from the same u_n and compares them block for block).

Why the mountain stays low there. These graphs are non-amenable: there is a c > 0 with |\partial K| \ge c |K| for every finite set of cells K, where \partial K is the set of cells next to K but not in it [4]. With r = 1, the available sites are D_n \cup \partial D_n, so each step lands on a new site with probability |\partial D_n| / (|D_n| + |\partial D_n|) \ge c/(1+c), whatever happened before. So on average at least a fraction c/(1+c) of the steps widen the base, which grows in proportion to n, unlike on the line, where the base after n steps is only of order \sqrt{n} wide.

How the cells are made. The mountain's branches reach far from the start (the base is spread out like a tree), further than the computer's numbers can tell neighboring cells apart by their positions: at distance 30 two neighbors differ only in about the 14th digit. So the page builds the graph from its combinatorics instead. Every cell has a kind, read off from which of its neighbors are closer to the start, as close, or farther, and how the cells around its corners sit. A hyperbolic tiling has only finitely many such kinds (its "cone types" [5]), and a cell's kind decides its children's. The page learns the kinds from the cells near the start, where positions are precise, checks them against geometry 14 units out, and then builds every cell from them with whole numbers only. For a few large tilings (big p and q) there are too many cells near the start to learn from, and the page builds them by position, which works up to about distance 23; it says so under the graph's options.

The pictures. The Poincaré disk \{|z| < 1\} and the upper half-plane \{\operatorname{Im} w > 0\} are two pictures of the same plane: w = i(1+z)/(1-z) carries one onto the other, taking hyperbolic lengths to hyperbolic lengths [6]. The spread-out picture of a tree is the usual radial drawing: the cells at distance k from the start sit on the circle of radius k, and every cell's children share its slice of the circle equally.
