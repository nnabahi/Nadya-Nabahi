About the model

Random walks is the name for a family of some of the most foundational models in Probability theory; the simplest versions can be introduced in high school, but their study can lead to a lifetime of deep mathematics. Each random walk model involves the space which the walker inhabits, as well as the way each subsequent step is chosen. Its ubiquity is to expected; as the most basic model for uncertain or unpredictable movement, random walks has been independently developed at several points in history. The term random walk was coined in 1905 by Karl Pearson while studying how mosquito swarms spread, ideas relating to it can be traced back to 1827 when botanist Robert Brown studied the movement of pollen grains suspended in water, and questions about random walks date even further back to 1656 when Blaise Pascal posed a problem on biased random walks to Pierre de Fermat.

The simplest form this model can take begins with a walker on a straight line, square grid, or their higher dimensional analogues, taking 1 step in any cardinal direction, with each direction equally likely. This model has been thoroughly studied, with questions ranging from how often the walker finds itself back in the starting position, if at all, to how long it takes to reach a certain distance from its starting point, to what kind of shapes its path takes.

The walk. Let e_1, \dots, e_d be the unit vectors of \mathbb{Z}^d (d = 1, 2, 3), and give each of the 2d directions \pm e_i a weight w_{\pm i} \ge 0, not all 0. The walk is S_0 = 0 and S_n = S_{n-1} + X_n, with X_1, X_2, \dots independent and

\mathbb{P}(X_n = \pm e_i) = \frac{w_{\pm i}}{\sum_j (w_{+j} + w_{-j})}.

All weights equal is the simple random walk. Otherwise the walk has a drift \mu = \mathbb{E} X_1 \ne 0 (or no drift but different variances, e.g. w_{+1} = w_{-1} = 2, w_{+2} = w_{-2} = 1).

Random numbers. Step n uses one uniform U_n \in (0, 1), made from the seed and n only, and goes in the first direction whose running total of probabilities passes U_n. So with the same seed, moving a weight only changes the steps whose U_n lies near a cut (the same coupling as the p slider of Bernoulli percolation).

Distance. For the simple random walk, \mathbb{E}|S_n|^2 = n in every dimension (the steps are uncorrelated with mean 0 and |X_k| = 1), which is the dashed \sqrt n. By Donsker's invariance principle [1], S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}/\sqrt{n} tends to a Brownian motion, so the largest distance M_n = \max_{k \le n} |S_k| also grows like \sqrt n; in 1D, \mathbb{E} \max_{k \le n} S_k \sim \sqrt{2n/\pi} by the reflection principle [2, Ch. III]. It wanders, though: in 1D, \limsup_n |S_n| / \sqrt{2 n \log \log n} = 1 almost surely, the law of the iterated logarithm [3], and \liminf_n M_n / \sqrt{n / \log \log n} = \pi/\sqrt 8 almost surely [4]. With a drift, M_n / n \to |\mu| by the strong law of large numbers.

Range and returns. For the simple random walk, the number of sites visited, R_n, grows like \sqrt{8n/\pi} in 1D, \pi n / \log n in 2D, and (1 - F) n in 3D, where F \approx 0.3405 is the chance of ever coming back to the origin [5, 6, 7] (the dashed lines; in 2D the approach is slow, with corrections of relative size \log\log n / \log n). The walk comes back to its start infinitely often in 1D and 2D, and only finitely often in 3D: Pólya's theorem [8]. The dashed line on the returns chart is the exact expected number of returns by step n, \sum_{k \le n/2} \mathbb{P}(S_{2k} = 0), with \mathbb{P}(S_{2k} = 0) = \binom{2k}{k} 4^{-k} in 1D and \big(\binom{2k}{k} 4^{-k}\big)^2 in 2D [2, Ch. XIV]; these grow like \sqrt{2n/\pi} and (\log n)/\pi. In 3D it is the limit F/(1-F) \approx 0.516. With any drift, every dimension has finitely many returns.

Convex hull. The hull of the walk is the smallest convex set holding S_0, \dots, S_n: a polygon (in 3D a polyhedron) whose sides are straight lines between visited sites. In 1D it is the interval from the lowest to the highest site. Spitzer and Widom [9] showed that in the plane its perimeter L_n has \mathbb{E} L_n = 2 \sum_{k=1}^n \mathbb{E}|S_k| / k, which for the simple random walk grows like 2\sqrt{\pi n}, matching the Brownian hull's \sqrt{8\pi t} [10] at t = n/2. The area A_n grows like \pi n / 4, from the Brownian hull's mean area \pi t / 2 [11]. With a drift both change: L_n \sim 2|\mu| n [12]. The page keeps the hull up to date as the walk visits new sites: a new site inside the hull changes nothing, and otherwise the hull of the old corners and the new site is found with Andrew's monotone chain [13]; in 3D, the hull of the lowest and highest site over each (x, y) is found with Quickhull [14].

Holes. In 2D, let K_n be the union of the closed unit squares around the visited sites (on another tiling, of the visited cells' closed polygons). Its holes are the bounded components of \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus K_n, and their number is the first Betti number \beta_1(K_n) (\beta_0 = 1 always, since the path is connected). A hole is born at the step that closes it off, or splits an older one, and dies when its last cell is visited; at a split the piece that dies last keeps the old birth (the elder rule of persistent homology [15]). They are found exactly, as in Eden Growth.

Fractal shape. Seen from far away, the walk's path is a Brownian path, which has Hausdorff dimension 2 in the plane [16]: it covers area, but leaves holes at every scale. For planar Brownian motion, the number of holes of area at least \varepsilon is about 2\pi / (\varepsilon \log^2 \varepsilon) as \varepsilon \to 0 [17], a power law (with a log correction), which the log-log hole sizes chart shows. The outer boundary of the Brownian path (its "frontier") has dimension 4/3, as Mandelbrot conjectured and Lawler, Schramm and Werner proved [18]; at the walk's scale \sqrt n that suggests an outer boundary of about (\sqrt n)^{4/3} = n^{2/3} side lengths (the dashed slope). That the walk itself follows this exponent is the expected answer from the scaling limit; this page doesn't claim it is proved for the walk. The outer boundary length here is the number of sides between a visited cell and an empty cell that can reach far away, worked out at steps about 10% apart.

Other tilings. In 2D the Lattice menu puts the walk on a tiling of the plane by regular polygons with sides of length 1 that repeats in two directions: the 11 where every corner looks the same, the 20 with two kinds of corner [19, Sections 2.1 and 2.2], or any tiling typed as a code in GomJau-Hogg notation [20] (see how codes work). The cells are its tiles, neighbors when they share a side, or its corners, neighbors when a side joins them. Each step goes to one of the cell's neighbors, all equally likely: the simple random walk on the graph of the cells (there are no weights). Distances and the hull go by the middles of the cells, in side lengths. Since the graph repeats in two directions, the walk looks like the one on \mathbb{Z}^2 from far away: the chance of being back at the start after 2n steps is of order 1/n [21], so the expected number of returns by step n grows like a constant times \log n. That sum is infinite, so the walk comes back infinitely often [22, Chapter 2]. The constants change from tiling to tiling, which is why the dashed theory lines hide. On a tiling with more than one kind of cell, the cells with more neighbors are visited more: in the long run the walk spends time at each cell in proportion to its number of neighbors [22, Chapter 2].

Penrose tilings. The menu also has Penrose's three tilings [23, 24]: pentagons, stars, boats and diamonds (P1), all with sides 1; kites and darts (P2), with sides 1 and \varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2; and thick and thin rhombs (P3), with sides 1. They never repeat: no shift moves one onto itself [19, Section 10.3]. So the walk is on the whole plane, starting at the cell nearest the tiling's point of five-fold symmetry, and the page builds the tiling as the walk reaches farther out, up to 160 side lengths from that point each way; the walk stops when it gets near there. The cells are the tiles, or for P2 and P3 the corners. A pentagon of P1 in the notch of a star or a boat shares two sides with it but is one neighbor, not two: the corner between those sides touches no other tile, so the two cells really meet along one bent side. The walk still comes back infinitely often. Whether a walk is recurrent depends only on what the graph looks like from far away: two connected graphs with bounded degrees that are roughly isometric (distances in one are distances in the other up to constant factors and an added constant) are both recurrent or both transient [22, Section 2.6]. On a Penrose tiling the number of steps between two cells is, in that sense, the distance between their middles (the tiles come in finitely many shapes), so its graph is roughly isometric to \mathbb{Z}^2, which is recurrent [8].
