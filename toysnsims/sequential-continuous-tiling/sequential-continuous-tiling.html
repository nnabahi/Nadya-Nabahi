About the model

Drop a random point in the domain. If it lands on a tile already there, nothing happens. Otherwise a new tile grows around it, as big as it can, until it touches the edge of the domain or an earlier tile, and then it stays there for good. Repeat.

With disks in a disk this gives a random version of the Apollonian gasket: a few big disks, and ever smaller ones filling the gaps. The tile that stopped a new tile from growing is its parent. Tiles stopped by the edge are generation 1, their children generation 2, and so on.

Things to try: press Play; drag the number of attempts; pick another start from the list; type your own domain, tile or density (any extra letter, like s, gets a slider); click an empty spot to grow a tile there yourself; or change the colors. The picture always starts out showing all of the domain. Zoom in with the mouse wheel, a pinch, or the + and − buttons to see the tiny tiles, and drag to look around.

The model. S is a domain and T a tile, star-shaped around 0; rT + C is T scaled by r and moved to C. The centers C_1, C_2, \dots are independent, with density proportional to f on S. With K_{n-1} the union of the first n-1 tiles,

R_n = \sup\{\, r \ge 0 : rT + C_n \subseteq \overline{S} \setminus \operatorname{int} K_{n-1} \,\},

which is 0 when C_n lands in an earlier tile, and tile n is D_n = R_n T + C_n. For S = T the unit disk this is

\begin{aligned} R_n &= \max\big(0,\ \min(1 - |C_n|,\ d_n)\big), \\ d_n &= \min_{k < n} \big(|C_n - C_k| - R_k\big), \end{aligned}

a random version of the Apollonian gasket [1, 2]; growing until blocked is the same idea as random sequential adsorption [3]. Your s model, C_n = \lambda_n^s \zeta_n with \lambda_n uniform on [0, 1] and \zeta_n uniform on the circle, has P(|C_n| \le \rho) = \rho^{1/s}, so its density in the plane is proportional to (x^2 + y^2)^{(1/s - 2)/2}; at s = 1/2 it is uniform.

Parents and generations. The parent of tile n is what it touches: the edge of S, or an earlier tile. Touching two at once has probability 0, so the parents make a tree with the edge as its root. The degree of a tile is its number of children plus one (for its parent); the degree of the edge is its number of children.

How R_n is computed. Let g(v) = \min\{\lambda : v \in \lambda T\}, the gauge of T; for the disk g(v) = |v|, for the square \max(|v_1|, |v_2|). A point y is inside rT + C exactly when g(y - C) < r, so R_n is the smallest g(y - C_n) over the points y on the edge of S and on the edges of earlier tiles. When T is convex and symmetric, aT + bT = (a+b)T [4, Section 3.1] and g is a norm [5, Section 15], so the distance to tile k is exactly g(C_n - C_k) - R_k, the disk formula with g in place of |\cdot|.

On the computer. T becomes a polygon with 4096 corners, found by binary search on its formula. The edge of S is traced by marching squares [6] on a 512 × 512 grid and refined at curves and corners. Nearby tiles are found with nested grids, each 4 times finer than the last (a hierarchical grid [7]), so each attempt costs about the same however many tiles there are. The centers are drawn by rejection sampling [8]: each grid square gets a ceiling for f, a square is picked in proportion to its ceiling, then a point in it is kept with probability f / \text{ceiling}. The check page, sequential-continuous-tiling-check.html, compares all of this with your formula and with known answers.
