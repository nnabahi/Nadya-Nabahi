About the model

Rectangular tiles are packed into a grid of cells, with no overlaps and nothing sticking out. With gaps allowed, empty cells are fine, but only where no tile fits: the packing is maximal. Without gaps, every cell must be covered (a tiling).

The goal is a uniformly random packing: every possible packing equally likely. There are far too many to list, so the sim mixes a packing one small piece at a time. Each move drops a disk at a random spot, removes the tiles it touches, and refills that spot in one of the allowed ways, chosen at random. After many moves, the packing looks like a uniform one.

The cells can also be triangles or hexagons. Then the tile is two neighboring cells: on triangles a rhombus. Rhombi tiling a hexagon look like a pile of cubes in a corner, seen from far away.

Each tile can also get a weight. Then a packing's chance is proportional to the product of its tiles' weights: a heavier tile shows up more often. All weights 1 is the uniform packing.

Things to try: switch between the presets; turn gaps off; make the mean radius bigger or smaller (the picture it ends up near stays the same, only the speed changes); color by direction; or switch to "Average over time". Click a spot to make one move there yourself. Slide a tile's weight while it runs and watch that tile take over or die out. Pick the Aztec diamond and look at the average to see the arctic circle, or pick Triangles and watch the empty corner fill with cubes. Zoom in with the mouse wheel, a pinch, or the + and − buttons, and drag to look around; on the torus, zoomed out, it shows several times side by side.

The domain is a finite set D of cells of \mathbb{Z}^2 (on a torus, of (\mathbb{Z}/W\mathbb{Z}) \times (\mathbb{Z}/H\mathbb{Z})); cell (x, y) is the unit square centered at (x, y). A packing is a set of rectangles from the tile list (turned by a quarter turn if rotations are allowed), each a union of cells of D, no two sharing a cell. With gaps allowed, the state space \Omega is the set of maximal packings (no tile fits in the uncovered cells); without gaps, the set of tilings. \Omega is finite, so the uniform distribution \pi(\sigma) = 1/|\Omega| exists.

One move from a packing \sigma:

  1. Pick a point c uniformly in the union of the cells, and a radius r \sim \mathrm{Exp} with mean \bar r. Let B = B(c, r).
  2. Let T be the tiles of \sigma that meet B, and E the empty cells that meet B. The region is R = \bigcup T \cup E.
  3. Let S(\sigma, B) be the packings \tau \in \Omega that agree with \sigma outside R, whose tiles inside R all meet B, and whose empty cells inside R all meet B.
  4. Move to a uniformly random element of S(\sigma, B). \sigma itself is in it.

Why it is uniform. For \tau \in S(\sigma, B), the same disk on \tau meets exactly the new tiles and frees exactly the same region, so S(\tau, B) = S(\sigma, B). Hence, for each B, the move is a uniform choice within a block of a partition of \Omega, and

P(\sigma, \tau) = \mathbb{E}_B\!\left[\frac{\mathbf{1}\{\tau \in S(\sigma, B)\}}{|S(\sigma, B)|}\right] = P(\tau, \sigma).

A symmetric transition matrix satisfies detailed balance with respect to the uniform distribution, which is therefore stationary [1, Proposition 1.20]. With positive probability B covers every cell; then S(\sigma, B) = \Omega, so the chain is irreducible, and P(\sigma, \sigma) > 0 makes it aperiodic; so it converges to \pi from any start [1, Theorem 4.9].

Skipped moves. If |S(\sigma, B)| is larger than the limit set in Options, or listing it takes too long, the move does nothing. Both depend only on the block, not on \sigma within it, so P stays symmetric and \pi stationary. (Irreducibility is then no longer guaranteed by the argument above, which needed the whole-domain disk.)

Weights. Each tile t may have a weight \lambda_t \ge 0. A tile of weight 0 is removed from the tile list. The target is then \pi(\sigma) \propto w(\sigma) = \prod_{\text{tiles } t \in \sigma} \lambda_t, and step 4 moves to \tau \in S(\sigma, B) with probability w(\tau) / \sum_{\rho \in S(\sigma, B)} w(\rho) (a heat-bath move [1, Section 3.3]). The tiles outside R are the same for the whole block, so only the tiles inside R matter. Since the block and its sum are the same from \sigma and from \tau, w(\sigma) P(\sigma, \tau) = w(\tau) P(\tau, \sigma): detailed balance for \pi. All weights 1 is the uniform case above.

Listing the refills. S(\sigma, B) is listed by a backtracking exact-cover search over the cells of R in a fixed order, Algorithm X [2]: at the first undecided cell, try each allowed tile covering it, or leave it empty if allowed. Maximality only has to be checked for tile positions using a cell of R. One refill is kept by reservoir sampling [3] (with weights: the k-th refill found replaces the kept one with probability its weight over the total weight so far), so the list is never stored.

The start. With gaps, the tile positions are tried in a uniformly random order and each one that fits is placed, which gives a maximal packing. Without gaps, a tiling is built directly: strips of one tile height across a box, or 1-tall tiles along each row of a drawn domain. This avoids a search (deciding whether a region can be tiled is NP-complete even for simple tiles [4]), so it can fail when a tiling exists.

Triangles and hexagons. The cells can also be the triangles or the hexagons of side 1 that tile the plane. Then the only tile is a pair of neighboring cells, in 3 directions, and a cell touches the disk when some point of it is in the disk; the move and the proof above don't change. A tiling of a hexagon of triangles by rhombi is a lozenge tiling, and it is the same as a pile of cubes in a box, seen along the box's diagonal. The hexagon with sides a, b, c has \prod_{i=1}^{a}\prod_{j=1}^{b}\prod_{k=1}^{c} \frac{i+j+k-1}{i+j+k-2} of them [7], and a uniformly random one is frozen near the corners, outside the circle inscribed in the hexagon, and random inside it [8]. Without gaps the start is each row cut into pairs, or on the hexagon the empty corner (3 big rhombi, each filled with rhombi of one direction). The chain starts far from a typical tiling, so on a big hexagon the circle takes many moves to appear: moves that change the tiling only near the disk move the picture slowly, like heat spreading (for the similar chain on domino tilings, see [9]).

Checks. On tiny domains every packing is listed and the chain visits each about equally often (or in proportion to its weight); domino counts agree with Kasteleyn's formula [5]; and dominoes on an Aztec diamond show the arctic circle [6]. These tests are on a separate check page.
