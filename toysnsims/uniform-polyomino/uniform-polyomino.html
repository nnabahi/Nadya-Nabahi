About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

Polyominoes. A polyomino with N cells is a set of N squares of the grid \mathbb{Z}^2, joined across shared sides [1]. Two are the same when one is a shift of the other; a turn or a flip gives a different one (“fixed” polyominoes), and holes are allowed. There are 1, 2, 6, 19, 63, 216, 760, 2725, … of them with N = 1, 2, 3, \dots cells [2, 3]; no formula is known, and the count grows like \lambda^N with \lambda \approx 4.06 [4].

The two perimeters. For a polyomino P, the edge perimeter L(P) is the number of sides between a cell of P and a cell outside it, and the site perimeter T(P) is the number of cells outside P that share a side with it. The page aims at the law

\pi(P) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp\big( b\, L(P) + s\, T(P) \big),

with Z the sum of the same over all polyominoes with N cells. At b = s = 0 it is uniform.

Cycles. Join two cells of P when they share a side. Each cell has 4 sides, each shared side is counted by both its cells, and the rest make up the edge perimeter, so 4N = 2E + L where E is the number of shared sides. The graph is connected, so its number of independent cycles (its cycle rank) is E - N + 1 = N + 1 - L/2 [5, Section 1.9]. So the heat b on the edge perimeter is the same as the heat -2b on the cycles: a short perimeter means many cycles. A cycle need not go around a hole: every 2 \times 2 block of cells is one.

Holes. A hole is a group of empty cells, joined across sides, that P cuts off from the outside. Think of each cell as a closed square, so two cells that touch only at a corner are joined there; then the number of holes is the first Betti number \beta_1 of the union of the squares [6, Theorem 3.44], and since the union is one piece, \beta_1 = 1 - \chi, where the Euler characteristic \chi is the number of corners minus the number of sides plus the number of squares [6, Theorem 2.44]. That is how the page counts them. For the uniform polyomino, the expected number of holes grows in proportion to N: it lies between C_1 N and C_2 N for all large N [7, Theorem 3].

Percolation. In site percolation each cell of \mathbb{Z}^2 is full with chance p, independently. The chance that the full cells joined to the origin make a shift of P is N p^N (1-p)^{T(P)}: the origin is one of its N cells, its cells are full, and its site perimeter is empty. So given that this cluster has N cells, it is a polyomino drawn from \pi with b = 0 and s = \log(1 - p) [7, Section 3.4]. The page shows the p that s stands for, and the button sets p to the critical point p_c \approx 0.59274621, known only from simulations [8], where a cluster first can be infinite.

The move. One move, from the polyomino P:

  1. Draw n uniformly from 1, \dots, K (at most N - 1).
  2. Take out n cells X of P, all choices equally likely, leaving R = P \setminus X.
  3. The touch rule: if some cell of X shares no side with R, stop (nothing changes).
  4. Put back n cells Y of the site perimeter \partial R of R (the empty cells next to it, which by the touch rule include every cell of X), all choices equally likely. Call the result Q = R \cup Y.
  5. If Q is not one piece, stop. Otherwise move to Q with chance \min\big(1, e^{b (L(Q) - L(P)) + s (T(Q) - T(P))}\big).

With n = 1 the touch rule always holds (a polyomino with at least 2 cells has no cell on its own), and this is Roldán's chain [7, Appendix A.1]; with b = 0 it is his percolation chain.

Why it is right. Given n, the move goes from P to Q through one leftover R with chance 1 \big/ \big(\binom{N}{n} \binom{|\partial R|}{n}\big), and it can go through R exactly when R has N - n cells and both P \setminus R and Q \setminus R lie in \partial R. Neither the chance nor the condition changes when P and Q swap, so summing over n and R, the chance of proposing Q from P equals that of proposing P from Q. With a symmetric proposal, the Metropolis rule in step 5 keeps \pi [9, 10; 11, Section 3.2]. Without the touch rule this fails: a cell of X away from R can be taken out, but never put back through the same R, since it is not in \partial R; the model check shows the bias this makes. Moves with n = 1 alone can reach every polyomino from every other [7, Theorem 8], and some moves change nothing, so the chain forgets where it started and its law tends to \pi [11, Theorem 4.9].

How fast. For a close cousin of the n = 1 move, from Stauffer [12] (move to a uniform pick among the polyominoes one cell swap away, with a Metropolis–Hastings correction), Coupier, Gheysens and Wicker prove that, from any start, the chain is within \epsilon of uniform after

14\, N^{4 \lceil \alpha \ln N \rceil + 12} \big(\ln a_N + \ln \epsilon^{-1}\big)

moves, where \alpha \approx 3.1028 and a_N is the number of polyominoes with N cells [13, Proposition 2]. That bound is for their move, not this page's, and it is far bigger than what runs show: with N = 200, the numbers on the charts stop drifting away from the straight line after a few hundred thousand moves. Larger K makes bigger changes per move, but more of its moves are turned down.

How the page runs it. The chain runs in a second thread. The cells sit on a grid with some room around them, made again whenever the polyomino drifts near its edge; the grid keeps, for every square, how many cells it touches, so the two perimeters change by a few additions per cell moved. Whether Q is one piece is checked from the cells next to X: first inside a small box around X, then, only if needed, through the whole polyomino, with one search from each of those cells at a time, stopping as soon as all have met or one is stuck. Every random number comes from the seed and the move's number, so the same seed gives the same run.
