About the model

(The simple explanation is coming.)

The model. The domain is a finite connected graph G = (V, E): the cells, joined to the 4 neighbors they share a side with (on other lattices, see below). A spanning tree of G is a set of edges T \subseteq E that joins every two cells by exactly one path: it has no cycles and reaches every cell. The uniform spanning tree is a spanning tree chosen uniformly at random among all of them. How many there are is given by Kirchhoff's matrix-tree theorem [3]; the check page uses it.

Wilson's algorithm [1]. Pick a root r; at first the tree is just r. Take a cell not in the tree, and run a simple random walk from it (each step to a uniformly random neighbor) until it hits the tree. Erase each loop of the walk as soon as it closes, and add what is left, a path with no loops, to the tree. Repeat until every cell is in the tree. The tree is then exactly uniform, whatever cells the walks start from and in whatever order [1; 2, Theorem 4.1]. The sim shows it step by step: the white line is the walk with its loops erased, and the black lines are the tree. It follows that the path in the uniform spanning tree between two cells has the law of the loop-erased random walk from one to the other [2, Corollary 4.3; 4].

Colors [2, Section 4.1 and Figures 4.3, 4.4]. Picture each cell x holding a stack of random neighbors S^x_1, S^x_2, \dots, and the walk always leaving x along the next one in its stack. Wilson's algorithm then pops cycles off the stacks until the top neighbors make a tree. The color of x is the place in its stack of the tree's edge from x: the number of times the walks stepped out of x. With the stacks fixed, neither the tree nor the colors depend on the order the walks are run in, and the colors are independent of the tree [2, Lemma 4.2 and Section 4.1]. Lyons and Peres note that they seem to have a fractal nature as the grid gets finer, which no one has yet explained [2, Section 4.1].

Distances [2, Figures 4.5, 4.6]. "Distance to the root" is the number of tree edges between each cell and the root, and "Distance to the blue path" the number of tree edges between each cell and the path in the tree from the other end to the root. Dragging the root on a finished tree keeps the tree and only changes which way its edges point: the uniform spanning tree does not depend on the root [1; 2, Theorem 4.1]. In 3D, each cell gets a stack that tall. In an n \times n box, the tree distance from one corner to the opposite one grows like n^{5/4}: conjectured by Guttmann and Bursill from simulations [5], calculated by Duplantier [6] and Majumdar [7] with non-rigorous conformal field theory, and proved in a form by Kenyon [8] (see [2, Section 4.1]).

Other lattices. The Lattice menu puts the cells on a tiling of the plane by regular polygons with sides of length 1 that repeats in two directions: the 11 where every corner looks the same, the 20 with two kinds of corner [9, Sections 2.1 and 2.2], or any tiling typed as a code in GomJau-Hogg notation [10] (see how codes work), or on one of Penrose's tilings. The cells are either the tiles, neighbors when they share a side, or the corners, neighbors when a side joins them. Wilson's algorithm works the same on any finite connected graph [1].

Hyperbolic plane or tree. Here G is the ball B_R = \{ v : d(o, v) \le R \} of a regular tiling \{p, q\} of the hyperbolic plane (1/p + 1/q < 1/2; neighbors share a side) or of the regular tree of degree d, with only the edges inside the ball. The root starts at the middle cell o. (On a tree, B_R is already a tree, so its only spanning tree is itself.)

Several pieces. If the domain is in several pieces, only the piece with the root has a spanning tree, so the other cells are left out.
